## Chapter 1: Play well (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did a carpenter's workshop make the toy of the century?"]

[REC 02 | 1930s | A 1930s Danish carpenter's workshop with wooden toy ducks and trucks lined up on a bench, wood shavings on the floor, snow outside a small window | A carpenter's hands sand a wooden duck and set it down in the row; camera static, medium | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Ole Kirk Christiansen and the early Lego workshop in Billund, 1930s to 1950s | Ole Kirk Christiansen Billund]

It starts in 1932, in a tiny village in Denmark called Billund, with a carpenter named Ole Kirk Christiansen. The Depression had killed his furniture business, so he started making wooden toys. He named the company after two Danish words, leg godt. Play well.

[REC 03 | 1960s | Colourful plastic bricks pouring out of an injection-moulding machine into a bin in a 1960s factory, a worker in a white coat watching | A new batch drops and clatters into the bin; camera static, close | 5]

[ARCHIVE: 1958 Lego brick patent drawings (public record) | Lego brick patent 1958]

After the war, the company bought a plastic-moulding machine and started making bricks. In 1958, it patented the version we know: studs on top, tubes underneath, so the bricks clutch each other. Any brick made since then still fits.

SAM (study) {stacks two bricks together}: That's the whole idea. One system. Every piece works with every other piece. A brick from 1958 still fits a brick from this morning.

[ARCHIVE: Lego catalogues and ads, 1960s to 1980s | Lego catalogue 1970s]

For the next thirty years, Lego grew almost every single year. Town sets. Space sets. Castles. Pirates.

## Chapter 2: Everything except bricks (3:30 to 6:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Lego stop making Lego?"]

[REC 04 | 2000s | A cluttered 2002 toy store aisle crowded with action figures, electronic toys, branded clothing and boxed play sets, none of them building toys, bright fluorescent light, no logos | A child walks past the shelf without stopping; camera static, wide | 6]

Then, in the nineties, the world changed. Kids had video games. Toy shops were being squeezed by big chains. And the patent on the brick ran out, so other companies could make cheaper bricks that fit.

Lego's leaders got scared. They decided kids were getting bored of building. So Lego started making everything else.

[ARCHIVE: Lego diversification products 1999 to 2003: Galidor, Jack Stone, Lego clothing, Legoland parks, Lego video games (product photos) | Lego Galidor; Lego Jack Stone]

Theme parks. Clothes. Watches. Video games. A TV show. Action figures that barely needed building at all. One line of toys came with big pre-made pieces, so a kid could finish a set in a few minutes.

SAM (location) {picks up a large, chunky pre-made toy piece from the table and frowns at it}: Here's the thing. Lego decided the brick was the problem. The brick was the only thing nobody else had.

[REC 05 | 2000s | A warehouse wall of thousands of small labelled plastic bins, each holding a different coloured part, a worker on a rolling ladder searching for one | The worker pulls out a bin, checks it, puts it back, moves the ladder; camera static, wide | 6]

Inside the company, the number of different pieces exploded. Thousands of new shapes and colors, many used in just one set. Every new mould cost money. Every new color meant more inventory. Some sets cost more to make than they sold for.

## Chapter 3: The edge (6:30 to 8:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How close did Lego come to the end?"]

[GFX: Bar chart, Lego profit and loss, 1998 to 2004, ending in a deep red bar]

In 1998, Lego lost money for the first time in its history. It recovered for a bit, then fell harder. In 2003 and 2004, it lost hundreds of millions of dollars, and sales were dropping fast.

[REC 06 | 2000s | A finance office at night, a man in a shirt staring at a spreadsheet on a monitor with his head in his hands, seen from behind, desk lamp on | He rubs his eyes and leans closer to the screen; camera static from behind | 6]

Later, people inside described how bad it really was. The company was burning through cash and had debts it couldn't easily pay. Some feared it might have to be sold, or broken up.

SAM (study) {leans forward}: Now watch this. This is the moment where most of my stories go wrong. The company panics, cuts the wrong thing, sells the best piece, and the countdown begins.

Lego did something else.

## Chapter 4: The outsider (8:30 to 11:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Who saved Lego?"]

[ARCHIVE: Jørgen Vig Knudstorp photos, 2004 onward | Jørgen Vig Knudstorp]

In 2004, the family that owned Lego handed the top job to a thirty-five-year-old named Jørgen Vig Knudstorp. He wasn't a toy man. He was a former management consultant with a PhD in economics who had only been at the company a few years.

[REC 07 | 2000s | A young executive in an open-collar shirt walking a toy factory floor with a notebook, seen from behind, past moulding machines and bins of bricks | He stops by a machine, writes something in the notebook, keeps walking; camera follows at shoulder height | 6]

The first thing he did was tell everyone the truth. In meetings he described Lego as being on a burning platform. Then he started cutting. The number of different pieces was cut roughly in half. The theme parks were sold. The video game studio and other side businesses were shut down or licensed out.

SAM (location) {sweeps the chunky pre-made piece off to one side and pulls a tray of plain bricks toward him}: And then he asked the question that saved the company. What are we actually good at?

[REC 08 | 2000s | A convention hall full of adult hobbyists showing huge brick-built city layouts on tables, visitors photographing them, bright hall lighting | A man adjusts a tiny brick train on a layout while visitors lean in; camera handheld, wide | 6]

Lego went and watched kids play. It also started listening to a group it had mostly ignored: adult fans. Grown-ups who built giant cities and spaceships in their spare time, and had been hacking Lego's robot kits on their own.

[REC 09 | 2000s | A design meeting where adult designers sit on the floor of a bright office building with bricks alongside two children, everyone laughing | A child holds up a small model to a designer, who studies it seriously; camera handheld, low | 6]

SAM (location) {holds up a single brick}: And the answer they all gave was embarrassingly simple. They wanted bricks.

## Chapter 5: Brick by brick (11:30 to 13:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did Lego become the biggest toy company on Earth?"]

[REC 10 | 2010s | A parent and child at a kitchen table building a large spaceship set together, instruction booklet open (no readable text), warm 3000 K pendant light | The child clicks a piece into place and the parent high-fives them; camera static, medium | 6]

Lego went back to the brick, and built a system around it. Fewer pieces, used in more sets. Big licensed themes like Star Wars, done with real care. Sets designed for adults. A website where fans could submit ideas, and if enough people voted, Lego would make them.

[ARCHIVE: The Lego Movie (2014) press stills and box office coverage | The Lego Movie 2014]

[GFX: Line chart, Lego revenue and profit, 2004 to 2015, crossing Mattel around 2014]

Profits came back within a year or two, then kept climbing for a decade. In 2014 The Lego Movie was a hit. And around that time, Lego passed Mattel, the maker of Barbie, to become the biggest toy company in the world.
