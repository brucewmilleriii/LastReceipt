## The catalog desk (1:00 to 2:30)

[REC 03 | 1980s | A department store catalog pickup desk in 1985, a woman behind a wood-grain counter handing a boxed order to a customer, shelves of brown-paper packages behind her | She slides the box across; the customer signs a slip on a clipboard; camera static, medium | 6]

[ARCHIVE: 1980s Sears catalog desk and catalog covers (scans, check license) | Sears catalog desk 1980s; Sears Wish Book 1985]

First stop, right by the door. The catalog desk. In 1985, Sears still printed its big catalog, more than a thousand pages, and you could order from it by phone and pick up here a few days later. This was online shopping. It just took longer to load.

[REC 04 | 1980s | A thick department store catalog open on a kitchen table in 1985, a child circling items in pen, a glass of milk beside it, warm 3000 K kitchen light | The pen circles a toy and the child flips the page; camera static, top-down | 5]

And if it was autumn, there was the Wish Book. The Christmas catalog. Kids circled things in it for weeks.

## Appliances (2:30 to 4:30)

[REC 05 | 1980s | A long row of almond-coloured washing machines and dryers on display in a 1985 department store, lids open, tags on each (blank), a salesman in a short-sleeve shirt and tie leaning on one | The salesman lifts a lid and gestures inside for an unseen customer; camera dollies slowly along the row | 7]

Appliances. The heart of Sears. Kenmore washers and dryers in almond, harvest gold or white. A good Kenmore washer was around four hundred dollars. That's about a week and a half of take-home pay for a lot of families in 1985.

[REC 06 | 1980s | A 1985 kitchen appliance display with a fridge in almond finish, a microwave oven with a mechanical dial timer on a counter, a couple examining the microwave, fluorescent light | The man turns the dial and the microwave's light comes on; his wife laughs; camera static, medium | 6]

And the new thing everyone wanted: a microwave. A countertop microwave ran a couple of hundred dollars. People were still learning what you could put in one. Not foil. We all learned that one.

SAM (location) {rests a hand on a washer lid}: Here's the thing about Kenmore. It wasn't made by Sears. Other companies built them, and Sears put its name on them and stood behind them. That promise was the product.

## Hardware (4:30 to 6:30)

[REC 07 | 1980s | A department store hardware aisle in 1985 with red steel toolboxes stacked high and walls of hand tools on pegboard, a father and his 10-year-old son looking at a socket set, fluorescent light, no logos | The father opens the socket set case and shows his son; camera static at waist height | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Craftsman tool ads and catalog pages, 1980s | Craftsman tools 1980s ad]

Your dad's stop. Craftsman tools. Rows of red toolboxes, wrenches, socket sets. And the famous promise: if a Craftsman hand tool ever broke, bring it back and they'd give you a new one. No receipt. Forever.

[REC 08 | 1980s | A hardware counter where a clerk takes a broken wrench from a customer and hands him a brand-new one from a drawer, no paperwork, 1985 | The clerk drops the broken wrench in a bin and hands over the new one; both nod; camera static, close | 5]

A big mechanic's set might run over a hundred dollars. A single wrench, a few dollars. And people bought them knowing they'd hand them down to their kids. A lot of them did.

[REC 09 | 1980s | A department store automotive area in 1985 with car batteries lined up on a low shelf, a mechanic in coveralls carrying one to a service bay door, oil-stained concrete beyond | He hefts the battery and pushes through the swing door; camera static, wide | 6]

Around the corner, the auto center. DieHard batteries, tires, and a bay where they'd fix your car while you shopped.

## Electronics (6:30 to 8:30)

[REC 10 | 1980s | A 1985 department store electronics section with a wall of wood-grain console TVs and smaller TV sets all showing the same colourful bars pattern (no readable picture), a family watching, fluorescent light | The kids press closer to one TV; the father checks a price tag; camera static, wide | 6]

Electronics. A wall of televisions, some built into wooden cabinets like furniture. A nineteen-inch color TV was a few hundred dollars.

[REC 11 | 1980s | A front-loading video cassette recorder on a glass display shelf in 1985 with a VHS tape half inserted, chunky piano-key buttons, a hand pushing the tape in | The tape slides in with a clunk and the door flips shut; camera static, macro | 5]

And the big one: a VCR. In 1985 a VCR could still cost three or four hundred dollars, and lots of families were buying their first. Which meant, on Friday nights, a trip to the video store. You've seen our Blockbuster episode. You know how that ended.

[REC 12 | 1980s | A teenager in a 1985 store trying on a portable cassette player with foam headphones, nodding to music, a display of portable stereos behind her | She presses play and starts nodding; camera static, medium | 5]

A portable cassette player with headphones, the kind every teenager wanted, ran somewhere from thirty to sixty dollars.

## The toy department (8:30 to 11:00)

[REC 13 | 1980s | A 1985 department store toy department with shelves of soft dolls in boxes, robot toys, and board games, children running between the aisles, bright light, no logos | A girl lifts a boxed doll off the shelf and hugs it; camera handheld at child height | 6]

Now it's your turn. The toy department.

In 1985, the toy everyone was fighting over was a soft doll with a birth certificate and adoption papers. The year before, parents had literally fought in stores to get one. By 1985 you could usually find one, for around thirty dollars.

[REC 14 | 1980s | A shelf of transforming robot toys in boxes and a boy turning one from a robot into a truck, 1985 toy aisle, bright light | His hands fold the robot into a truck shape and he grins; camera static, close | 5]

Robots that turned into trucks. Action figures. Board games stacked to the ceiling.

[REC 15 | 1980s | A video game section in 1985 with a bargain bin of game cartridges marked down, a boy digging through it, an older console on display beside it | He pulls out a cartridge, reads the back, drops it back in; camera static, medium | 5]

SAM (location) {picks up a game cartridge from a bargain bin}: And here's a strange sight. Video games, in a bargain bin. In 1985, the home video game business had just crashed. Stores couldn't give the cartridges away.

SAM (location) {drops it back}: That October, a Japanese company launched a new console in New York, as a test. By the end of the decade, the bargain bin was gone.

## The portrait studio and the candy counter (12:30 to 14:00)

[REC 18 | 1980s | A small department store portrait studio in 1985, a family posed in front of a mottled blue backdrop, a photographer behind a big camera on a stand, umbrella lights | The flash fires; the youngest child blinks; everyone laughs; camera static from the side | 5]

Tucked in a corner, the portrait studio. A mottled blue backdrop, a photographer squeezing a squeaky toy, and a family trying not to blink. Somewhere in a drawer, you probably have one of these.

[REC 19 | 1980s | A department store candy counter in 1985 with warm nuts turning in a heated glass display case and bins of loose candy, a clerk scooping cashews into a white paper bag, warm 3000 K light | The scoop pours into the bag and the clerk folds the top; camera static, close | 5]

And then, on the way out, the candy counter. Warm nuts under a glass case. Cashews, Spanish peanuts, by the quarter pound. If you grew up near a Sears, you can probably smell it right now.

SAM (location) {holds a small white paper bag}: Here's where your three dollars of birthday money went. Every time.

## The credit desk (14:00 to 15:00)

[REC 20 | 1980s | A credit services desk in a 1985 department store, a woman filling out a paper application with a pen on a chain, a clerk waiting behind a low counter | She signs at the bottom and slides it back; camera static, medium | 5]

[ARCHIVE: 1985 to 1986 Discover card launch ads | Discover card 1986 launch]

One more stop. The credit desk. Sears had its own store card for decades. And around 1985, Sears was launching something new: a general credit card, called Discover. It still exists. Most people have no idea Sears started it.
