## Chapter 1: Friday night (0:50 to 4:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did everyone love a store they hated?"]

[REC 04 | 1990s | A crowded neighbourhood video rental store on a Friday night, aisles of VHS boxes face-out, families and teenagers browsing, blue and yellow wall colours, no logos | A teenager pulls a tape box off the wall and shows it to a friend; camera handheld at chest height, drifting down the aisle | 6]

[ARCHIVE: 1990s Blockbuster TV commercials and store exterior photos | Blockbuster Video store 1990s]

If you're over thirty-five, you can probably still smell it. Carpet, popcorn and plastic. Friday night, six thirty, and the whole neighborhood is in there, walking the walls, looking for a movie that isn't already gone.

Blockbuster started in Dallas in 1985, with an idea from a man named David Cook. At the time, video stores were small. A few hundred tapes, a guy behind a counter, a curtain in the back you didn't ask about. Cook came from the software business. He opened a store with thousands of titles and a computer system that tracked every single one.

[ARCHIVE: Wayne Huizenga portrait photos and newspaper clippings, late 1980s | Wayne Huizenga]

[GFX: US map, store dots multiplying from Dallas, 1985 to 1994]

It worked so well that it caught the eye of Wayne Huizenga, the man who'd built a garbage-hauling company called Waste Management into a giant. Huizenga didn't know movies. He knew how to open the same thing a thousand times. Under him, Blockbuster was opening a new store about every day.

By 1994, the media company Viacom paid more than eight billion dollars for it.

[REC 05 | 1990s | A hand sliding a VHS tape into a store's after-hours return slot at night, rain on the steel slot, sodium street light | The tape drops through the slot; rain drips off the edge; camera static, close-up | 5]

Now, here's what made the money. A new movie on tape cost the store a fortune, sometimes sixty or seventy dollars a copy. So stores never had enough copies of the hit everyone wanted. In the late nineties, Blockbuster cut a deal with the studios: give us the tapes cheap, and we'll share the rental money with you. Suddenly the walls were full of the new release.

[REC 06 | 1990s | Close-up of a thermal receipt curling out of a 1990s counter printer, store counter, fluorescent light | The receipt prints and curls; a cashier's hand tears it off; camera static | 5]

[GFX: Added in edit on the receipt: "LATE FEE $4.99"]

And then there was the other thing. The thing everybody hated.

SAM (study) {raises one finger on "late fees"}: Late fees. In the year 2000, Blockbuster collected roughly eight hundred million dollars in late fees. About sixteen cents of every dollar it made.

SAM (study) {sits back, a small dry smile}: Think about that. One of this company's biggest products was its customers' mistakes.

Remember that number. It comes back.

## Chapter 2: The meeting (4:00 to 7:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Blockbuster say no?"]

[ARCHIVE: Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph photos, late 1990s; early Netflix red envelope | Reed Hastings 2000; Netflix DVD envelope]

There's a famous story that Netflix exists because one of its founders, Reed Hastings, got hit with a forty-dollar late fee on a copy of Apollo 13. His co-founder, Marc Randolph, has said that story is more of a convenient legend. But it tells you something that it was believable. Everyone had a late fee story.

[REC 07 | 2000s | A red paper DVD mailer envelope sticking out of a suburban home mailbox, morning light 5600 K, dew on the post | A hand pulls the envelope from the mailbox; camera static | 5]

Netflix launched in 1998. By 2000 it had a clever model, a flat monthly price, no due dates, no late fees, and a serious problem. It was on track to lose more than fifty million dollars that year, and the dot-com bubble had just burst. Nobody was handing money to internet companies anymore.

So Hastings, Randolph and their finance chief, Barry McCarthy, went to Dallas. Their pitch was this: you run the stores, we'll run your online business. Buy us for fifty million.

Now, it's easy to laugh at Blockbuster here. But put yourself in that chair.

SAM (study) {spreads both hands on the desk}: You're running a company with six billion dollars in sales. Thousands of stores. And these guys, losing money mailing discs in envelopes, want fifty million.

SAM (study) {shrugs once}: In the year 2000, hardly anybody even owned a DVD player. Saying no wasn't crazy. Saying no was the sensible thing to do.

[ARCHIVE: Press coverage of the 2000 Blockbuster and Enron video-on-demand deal | Blockbuster Enron video on demand 2000]

And Blockbuster wasn't ignoring the future. That same year, it signed a twenty-year deal to deliver movies over the internet, straight to your TV. Video on demand, in the year 2000. The trouble was the partner it picked.

It was Enron.

[Beat.]

That deal fell apart within months. Enron, as you may know, had some other problems coming. That's a different episode.

So Blockbuster went back to what it knew. Stores. And for a few more years, that looked like the right call.

## Chapter 3: The crack (7:00 to 10:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "What was really wrong with Blockbuster?"]

[ARCHIVE: Early-2000s DVD bargain bins at big-box stores | DVD bargain bin 2003]

[GFX: Line chart, US households with a DVD player, 1998 to 2006]

Three things happened in the early 2000s, and none of them looked like an emergency.

First, DVDs got cheap. Not to rent. To buy. Walmart was selling new movies for fifteen dollars and old ones out of a bin for five. Why pay four dollars to borrow a movie for two nights when you could own it for a little more?

[REC 08 | 2000s | A couple in their thirties on a beige couch in a 2005 living room, tearing open a red DVD mailer, a bulky CRT TV in the background, table lamp 2800 K | She slides the disc out and hands it to him; camera handheld, slight drift | 6]

Second, that little mail company didn't die. Netflix went public in 2002. It reached a million subscribers in 2003. Its customers were keeping movies for three weeks and paying nothing extra, and they were telling everyone.

[ARCHIVE: Red DVD rental kiosks outside grocery stores, mid-2000s | Redbox kiosk]

Third, a red vending machine started showing up outside supermarkets and McDonald's. A dollar a night. It was called Redbox.

So Blockbuster was getting squeezed from three sides. Cheaper to buy. Easier by mail. Cheaper at the grocery store.

[GFX: Bar, Blockbuster debt after the 2004 split from Viacom, about $1 billion]

And right at that moment, its parent company decided to leave. In 2004, Viacom spun Blockbuster off into its own company, and on the way out, Blockbuster paid its shareholders a huge one-time dividend. To do that, it borrowed. Blockbuster walked into the hardest fight of its life carrying roughly a billion dollars in debt.

SAM (location) {walks two steps along an empty shelf and stops}: Here's the thing about debt. When business is good, nobody notices it. When business turns, it's the only thing anyone notices.

SAM (location) {taps the bare metal shelf once}: A company with debt doesn't get to make a three-year plan. It gets to make this quarter's payment.

[ARCHIVE: John Antioco photos and interviews | John Antioco Blockbuster]

The man running Blockbuster through all of this was John Antioco. He's the one who sat across from Netflix in that meeting. He'd turned around Taco Bell and Circle K before this, and he was not a fool. By 2004, he'd seen enough.

And he made two decisions that could have saved the company.

## Chapter 4: The comeback nobody remembers (10:00 to 13:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Did Blockbuster almost beat Netflix?"]

[ARCHIVE: Blockbuster Online launch ads, 2004; the blue mailer envelope | Blockbuster Online 2004]

Decision one. In 2004, Blockbuster launched Blockbuster Online. DVDs by mail, flat monthly price. A copy of Netflix, four years late, in a blue envelope.

[ARCHIVE: "The End of Late Fees" TV commercials, 2005 | Blockbuster end of late fees 2005 commercial]

Decision two was the brave one. On January first, 2005, Blockbuster ended late fees.

Remember that number? Hundreds of millions of dollars a year. Antioco walked into a room and said: we're going to stop charging it. The plan was expected to cost the company around four hundred million dollars, between the lost fees and the price of building the online service.

SAM (location) {turns from the shelf to face camera}: Now watch this. For about a year, it wasn't working. And then somebody at Blockbuster had the best idea in the history of the company.

[ARCHIVE: Total Access TV ads, 2006 | Blockbuster Total Access commercial]

[REC 09 | 2000s | A customer at a video store counter handing a blue mailer envelope to a clerk, who hands back a DVD case, 2006, fluorescent 4200 K, no logos | The envelope and the DVD case swap hands across the counter; camera static at counter height | 5]

It was called Total Access. If you rented by mail from Blockbuster, you didn't have to mail the movie back. You could walk into any store, hand over the envelope, and walk out with a new movie. Free. Right then.

Think about what that meant. Netflix's biggest weakness was the wait. You finished a movie on Friday night, and your next one showed up on Tuesday. Blockbuster had thousands of stores full of movies sitting on shelves. The thing everyone said was its dead weight was suddenly its weapon. Netflix couldn't copy it. Netflix didn't have a single store.

[GFX: Line chart, Blockbuster Online subscribers climbing in early 2007 while Netflix's line flattens]

And it worked. Blockbuster's online service added about a million subscribers in a matter of months, and passed two million. In the spring of 2007, Netflix did something it had almost never done. It lost subscribers.

[ARCHIVE: Reed Hastings interview clips, 2007 to 2008 | Reed Hastings 2007 interview]

Reed Hastings has said since that Blockbuster had them in a serious bind. There are accounts that Netflix even reached out about buying Blockbuster's online business just to end the fight.

So sit with that for a second. In early 2007, seven years after the laugh, the company that was losing was Netflix.

[REC 10 | 2000s | A boardroom with men in suits standing around a table in silhouette against a bright window, one throwing a folder of papers onto the table | Papers slide across the table; one man turns away to the window; camera static, wide | 6]

[Music drops out.]

Blockbuster had the stores. It had the brand. It had the momentum.

What it didn't have was a board that agreed with its own chief executive.

## Chapter 5: The fight (13:30 to 16:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Who actually killed Blockbuster?"]

[ARCHIVE: Carl Icahn news photos and headlines, 2005 | Carl Icahn 2005]

Back in 2005, a famous Wall Street investor named Carl Icahn had bought a large stake in Blockbuster. Icahn's specialty was simple: buy into a struggling company, get seats on the board, and push management to make the stock go up. He won those seats in a shareholder vote.

And from where Icahn sat, Antioco's plan looked like a bonfire. Ending late fees had cost a fortune. Total Access was costing more. Every time a customer swapped an envelope for a free movie in a store, Blockbuster lost money on it. The online service was growing, and it was burning cash to do it.

SAM (study) {rubs his thumb along the desk edge}: And to be fair to the board, they weren't wrong about the math. It was expensive.

SAM (study) {looks up into the lens}: This is the hard part of every one of these stories. The thing that saves you in five years is usually the thing that hurts you this quarter.

[GFX: Tug of war, "Spend to win" against "Protect profits"]

The tension finally snapped over something small. Antioco's bonus. For 2006, his contract entitled him to a bonus of several million dollars. The board decided to pay him a fraction of it. He fought it. They negotiated. And in 2007, in the middle of the one strategy that was working, John Antioco agreed to leave.

[ARCHIVE: Jim Keyes headlines, 2007 | Jim Keyes Blockbuster CEO]

His replacement was Jim Keyes, who had run 7-Eleven. Keyes was a store guy, and he made a store guy's decision. He raised prices on Total Access, pulled back spending on the online fight, and put the focus back on the stores. He talked about turning them into entertainment shops, selling snacks, games and gadgets.

[GFX: Line chart, Netflix subscribers resuming their climb after mid-2007; Blockbuster Online falling]

Netflix started growing again almost immediately.

[ARCHIVE: Netflix "Watch Instantly" launch coverage, January 2007 | Netflix Watch Instantly 2007]

And Netflix had used its near-death experience well. In January 2007, it had quietly launched a little feature that let you watch a movie instantly on your computer. No disc at all. Hardly anyone noticed. The catalog was small and the picture was rough.

It was called streaming.

[REC 11 | 2010s | Two employees in plain polo shirts unbolting grey metal shelves in an empty rental store, worn blue carpet, half the fluorescent tubes off | One lifts a shelf off its brackets and carries it out of frame; camera static, wide | 6]

So by 2008, here's where things stood. Blockbuster had a billion dollars of debt, thousands of leases, a mail service it had stopped feeding, and a boss betting on candy and video games. Netflix had no stores, no debt to speak of, and the future.

Then the financial crisis hit, and lenders stopped being patient.

## Chapter 6: The last store (16:30 to 18:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why is one Blockbuster still open?"]

[ARCHIVE: Bankruptcy headlines and store-closing signs, 2010 | Blockbuster store closing 2010]

On September 23rd, 2010, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy, owing about nine hundred million dollars. The satellite TV company Dish bought what was left at auction in 2011 for around three hundred and twenty million. A company that had sold for more than eight billion.

Dish tried for a while. But in early 2014, the last company-owned Blockbuster stores in America closed.

[GFX: Map, thousands of dots disappearing until one remains, in Bend, Oregon]

A few stores owned by local franchise holders hung on. In Alaska. In Australia. One by one, they went dark.

[ARCHIVE: The last Blockbuster in Bend, Oregon, exterior and interior photos | Blockbuster Bend Oregon]

[REC 12 | 2020s | A small-town strip-mall video store at dusk under tall pine trees in central Oregon, one lit sign (blank), a few tourists photographing the front with phones, no logos | A couple takes a selfie in front of the window and laughs; camera handheld from across the parking lot | 6]

Until there was one. Bend, Oregon. It's still there. It still rents movies. The computers behind the counter are ancient, and people fly in from around the world to stand in the aisles and remember what a Friday night used to feel like.

And Netflix? The company Blockbuster could have had for fifty million dollars went on to be worth well over a hundred billion.

It had the answer. It had the stores, the brand, and for a few months, the lead. And it fired the plan to protect the profits of a business that was already dying.
