## Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)

[REC 01 | 1990s | A huge two-storey bookstore with a café in 1998, people reading in deep armchairs, a student asleep over a textbook, warm 3000 K pendant light, rain on the windows, no logos | A woman turns a page and sips her coffee; a man walks past with a stack of books; camera static, wide | 6]

If you remember it, you remember the feeling. A bookstore so big you could get lost in it. Armchairs. Coffee. Nobody rushing you out. You could spend a whole Saturday in there and buy one paperback.

And the staff actually read. At a lot of Borders stores, new hires had to pass a test about books and authors before they got the job. You could ask someone for something you half-remembered, a blue cover, a war novel, the author's name started with an M, and they'd walk you to the shelf.

In 2001, that bookstore made a decision that sounds unbelievable now. It handed its entire website to Amazon.

## Chapter 1: The clever bookstore (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "What made Borders smarter than other bookstores?"]

[REC 02 | 1970s | A cosy 1971 college-town used bookshop, two young men in their twenties shelving books on tall wooden shelves, wooden floor, afternoon sun through a front window | One hands a book up to the other on a short ladder; camera static, medium | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Early Borders store in Ann Arbor, Michigan, 1970s to 1980s | Borders bookstore Ann Arbor 1970s]

Borders started in 1971 in Ann Arbor, Michigan, as a used bookshop run by two brothers, Tom and Louis Borders. Louis was the numbers guy. And he built something that made Borders different from every other bookstore in America.

[REC 03 | 1980s | A 1980s back office with a computer terminal and a dot-matrix printer feeding green-bar paper covered in columns of numbers, a stack of printouts on the desk | The printer chatters and the paper folds into a pile; camera static, close | 5]

A computer system that tracked what every store sold and predicted what each store would sell next. A store near a university got different books from a store in the suburbs. Borders could carry a huge range of titles, because the system told it which ones would actually move.

In the seventies and eighties, that was rare. Most bookstores ordered by gut feeling and whatever the publisher's sales rep pushed. Borders ordered by evidence. It knew that one store sold poetry and another sold boating manuals, and it stocked them that way. Publishers started asking Borders what was selling, because Borders knew before anyone else.

[ARCHIVE: Louis Borders and Webvan coverage, 1999 to 2001 | Louis Borders Webvan]

And here's a twist you won't believe. Years later, after he'd left the bookstore, Louis Borders started one of the most famous internet companies of the dot-com boom. An online grocery delivery service called Webvan. It raised huge amounts of money, built giant warehouses, and went bust in 2001. So one of the founders of Borders went all in on the internet, too early. And the company he left behind went in too late.

SAM (study) {taps the book}: Here's the thing. In the eighties, Borders was a bookstore with a data advantage. Hold onto that. It's the saddest part of this story.

[ARCHIVE: Borders superstores in the 1990s | Borders superstore 1990s]

In 1992, the brothers sold the company to Kmart. Yes, that Kmart, from our episode on its collapse, which also owned the Waldenbooks chain at the time. A few years later Kmart spun Borders off as its own company, and Borders grew fast. Giant superstores, with cafés, music sections and chairs.

Kmart had also owned Waldenbooks, and the two were spun off together.
Waldenbooks was the small bookstore in the mall, the one with the bargain table at the front and the bestsellers by the door. Borders was the opposite: big, serious, full of books you'd never heard of. Putting the two together under a discount store company was a strange marriage. Kmart never really understood either of them.

The superstores changed what a bookstore was. Before, a bookshop was a place you went to buy a book. Borders made it a place you went to spend an afternoon. Students did homework there. Couples went on first dates there. Musicians played in the café on Friday nights. Its only real rival at that size was Barnes and Noble, and through the nineties the two of them opened superstores across America, often putting small independent shops out of business along the way.

## Chapter 2: The deal (3:30 to 6:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why would a bookstore let Amazon run its website?"]

Meanwhile, a company in Seattle was selling books on the internet. It was losing money every year, and most people in the book business thought it was a fad.

Borders had a website of its own. It wasn't very good, and it lost money. So in 2001, Borders made a deal.

And honestly, you can see the reasoning. The dot-com crash had just happened. Internet companies were collapsing all over the place, including, as it happens, Louis Borders' own. Running a website meant warehouses, shipping, technology staff, all of it losing money. Amazon offered to take that headache away and pay Borders a commission. On a spreadsheet in 2001, it looked like a safe, sensible way to stop the bleeding.

Barnes and Noble looked at the same problem and chose the opposite. It kept building its own website, even while it lost money. That choice is going to matter.

[REC 05 | 2000s | Two executives in suits shaking hands across a conference table in 2001, shown from the shoulders down, documents and a laptop between them | The handshake ends and one taps the documents into a neat stack; camera static, close | 5]

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the Borders-Amazon partnership, 2001; Borders.com redirect screenshots (Wayback Machine) | Borders Amazon 2001 borders.com]

If you typed borders dot com into your browser, you'd land on a page run by Amazon. Amazon handled the website, the warehouses and the shipping. Borders got a cut of the sales.

SAM (location) {walks between two rows of bare shelves}: Now watch this. Borders gave Amazon its online customers. Every one of them. Their emails, their orders, their habits. And Amazon learned more about Borders' customers than Borders did.

SAM (location) {stops and runs a hand along a bare shelf}: The company that won by knowing which book each store would sell handed that knowledge to its rival.

[REC 06 | 2000s | Cardboard shipping boxes moving along a conveyor in a giant fulfilment warehouse, workers scanning them, bright LED light | A box tips down a chute and slides onto the next belt; camera static, high wide angle | 6]

The deal lasted about seven years. Borders didn't run its own website again until 2008.

[GFX: Timeline bar, 2001 to 2008: "borders.com run by Amazon" across seven years]

Think about what happened in those seven years. Online shopping went from strange to normal. People got used to one-click ordering, customer reviews and recommendations. Amazon learned all of that partly on Borders' customers. When Borders finally switched on its own website again, it was a newcomer in a business it had once been in early.

## Chapter 3: The wrong bets (6:30 to 9:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "What else went wrong at Borders?"]

[REC 07 | 2000s | A bookstore music section with long racks of CDs and a listening station, a few shoppers, 2003, fluorescent light | A shopper puts on headphones at the listening station and nods along; camera static, wide | 6]

While Amazon was learning how to sell online, Borders was making other bets. It gave a big chunk of its stores to CDs and DVDs, right before people stopped buying CDs and DVDs. It opened stores in other countries. It signed long, expensive leases on huge buildings. And to fund it all, it took on debt.

[ARCHIVE: Borders CD and DVD departments, 2000s | Borders music department]

The music bet is the one that stings. Walk into a Borders in 2003 and a huge part of the floor was CDs and DVDs, with listening stations where you could put on headphones and try an album. It was lovely. But that same year, people started buying songs one at a time online for under a dollar. The floor space that had been a draw became a dead zone.

[GFX: Bars, Borders floor space for music and DVDs vs US CD sales, 2000 to 2010]

SAM (study) {leans back}: Here's the hard part. Every one of those bets looked reasonable at the time. Music was big. Leases were cheap. The trouble is they all pointed backward.

## Chapter 4: The e-reader (9:30 to 12:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why didn't Borders build an e-reader?"]

[ARCHIVE: Amazon Kindle launch, November 2007; Barnes & Noble Nook launch, 2009 (product photos) | Kindle 2007; Nook 2009]

In 2007, Amazon launched the Kindle. Two years later, Borders' big rival, Barnes and Noble, launched its own e-reader, the Nook, and pushed it hard in its stores.

[REC 08 | 2000s | A commuter on a train in 2009 reading on a small grey e-reader, the person next to them reading a paperback, winter morning light through the window | The commuter clicks to the next page; the neighbour glances over; camera static, medium | 6]

Borders didn't have one. It was late to e-books, late to its own website, and by now, short of money. In 2010 it started selling another company's e-reader in its stores. By then most people who wanted one already had one.

Here's why that mattered so much. An e-reader isn't just a gadget. It's a lock. Once you've bought fifty e-books for your Kindle, you're going to keep buying them from Amazon, because that's where your library lives. Every Kindle sold in 2008 and 2009 was a reader who might never walk into a bookstore again. Barnes and Noble understood that, and fought for its readers with the Nook. Borders, short on cash and focus, watched.

[REC 09 | 2010s | A bookstore café with empty tables and chairs, a barista wiping the counter at 3 pm on a weekday, rain outside | The barista wipes, stops, looks around the empty café; camera static, wide | 6]

[GFX: Line chart, Borders revenue and losses, 2006 to 2010]

Sales fell year after year. In 2008 the financial crisis made the debt harder to carry. Publishers started worrying about getting paid. And then Borders started paying its bills late.

There were rescue attempts. Big investors put in money and lent more. Borders sold off its stores overseas to raise cash. Executives came and went, each with a plan to make the stores more modern, more digital, more like the future. But it's hard to build the future while you're paying off the past, and Borders was still paying rent on giant stores with half-empty music sections.

## Chapter 5: The last chapter (12:00 to 13:30)

[ARCHIVE: Borders bankruptcy and liquidation coverage, 2011; store-closing photos | Borders bankruptcy 2011 liquidation]

In February 2011, Borders filed for bankruptcy. It hoped to find a buyer. A deal fell through. In July, it announced it would close everything.

For a few weeks that summer there was hope. A private investment firm offered to buy the chain and keep many stores open. But the lenders didn't trust the plan, and worried the buyers might simply close the stores later and sell off what was left. The deal collapsed. Liquidation, the lenders decided, was the surer way to get their money back.

[REC 10 | 2010s | Shelves half empty in a large bookstore with blank "everything must go" banners hung from the ceiling, customers carrying tall stacks of discounted books to the registers | A man balances a stack of books under his chin and heads for the checkout; camera handheld, wide | 6]

[REC 11 | 2010s | A bookstore employee hand-writing a goodbye note on a sheet of paper and taping it to the inside of a front window, back to camera, evening light | She smooths the tape down and steps back to look at it; camera static from inside | 6]

By that September, the last Borders stores were dark. In some of them, staff left handwritten notes in the windows, thanking customers.

[ARCHIVE: Handwritten staff notes in Borders store windows, 2011 | Borders closing staff note]

Some of those notes were funny. Some were angry. Many were simply thank yous, from people who'd loved recommending books for a living. About eleven thousand people lost their jobs. And, in a detail that echoes our RadioShack story, the Borders customer list was sold too, to its old rival Barnes and Noble, with privacy rules attached after regulators stepped in.

Barnes and Noble survived. It had a rough decade. But in recent years it's actually been opening stores again, under a boss who gave each store's managers the freedom to choose books for their own neighbourhood.

SAM (study) {opens the hardback and closes it gently}: Each store stocking the books its own neighbourhood wants. Does that sound familiar? It's what Borders did in 1975.

[REC 12 | 2010s | A single book left lying on an otherwise empty bookshelf in a closed bookstore, the overhead lights switching off row by row toward camera | The last row of lights clicks off; camera static | 6]

## The verdict (13:30 to 15:00)

[GFX: Timeline recap: 1971 Ann Arbor shop, 1992 sold to Kmart, 1995 spun off, 2001 website handed to Amazon, 2007 Kindle, 2008 own website back, 2009 Nook, 2011 bankruptcy and closing]

Let's go back over it. Two brothers who built a bookstore that ran on data before anyone used that word. Superstores with armchairs and coffee that changed what a bookshop could be. A deal in 2001 that handed its online customers to its biggest future rival. Big bets on CDs and huge leases. And no e-reader when it mattered.

SAM (boardroom) {stands at the head of the table, a hardback in hand}: So what killed Borders? Amazon, of course. Everybody says Amazon.

SAM (boardroom) {sets the book on the table}: But Borders didn't lose to the internet. It outsourced it. It took the one thing it was best at, knowing its customers, and gave it to the company that would replace it.

[GFX: Red CLOSED stamp lands on the Borders storefront photo. One heavy thud.]

SAM (boardroom) {looks into the lens}: The lesson is short. Never outsource the part of your business that's going to matter most in ten years. Even if it's losing money today.
