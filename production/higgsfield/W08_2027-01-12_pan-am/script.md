## Chapter 1: The Clippers (0:45 to 4:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did a mail route become the world's airline?"]

[ARCHIVE: Juan Trippe portraits; early Pan Am Key West to Havana mail flights, 1927 | Juan Trippe; Pan American Airways 1927]

It began in 1927 with a ninety-mile mail route, from Key West to Havana. The man behind it was Juan Trippe, a young, ambitious ex-Navy pilot who believed airplanes would one day carry ordinary people across oceans.

[REC 02 | 1930s | A large four-engine flying boat taking off from a calm tropical bay in the 1930s, spray trailing from the hull, palm-lined shore | The flying boat lifts off the water and climbs; camera static on a long lens from the shore | 7]

[ARCHIVE: Pan Am Clipper flying boats, China Clipper 1935 | China Clipper 1935]

In the thirties, Pan Am flew giant flying boats it called Clippers. They landed on water, because there weren't runways in most of the places Pan Am wanted to go. In 1935, the China Clipper crossed the Pacific, hopping between islands where Pan Am had built its own bases. It took days. It was the future.

SAM (study) {leans forward}: Here's the thing about Pan Am. It didn't just fly to places. It built the places it flew to. Airports, weather stations, radio networks, even hotels.

## Chapter 2: The jet age (4:00 to 7:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Who invented the jumbo jet?"]

[ARCHIVE: Pan Am Boeing 707 inaugural service, 1958 | Pan Am Boeing 707 1958]

In 1958, Pan Am started flying the Boeing 707 from New York to Paris. The jet age had arrived, and Pan Am was out front.

[REC 03 | 1960s | Stewardesses in powder-blue uniforms with matching hats walking in a line through a bright 1960s airport terminal, travellers turning to look | They walk past a row of check-in desks, chatting; camera tracks with them at waist height | 6]

The stewardesses became famous. The blue globe logo was everywhere. Pan Am built a skyscraper on top of Grand Central Station in New York with its name across the top. When the movie 2001: A Space Odyssey showed a passenger spaceplane, it had Pan Am's logo on it.

[REC 04 | 1960s | A giant four-engine jumbo jet slowly rolling out of an enormous hangar into daylight for the first time in 1969, a crowd of workers watching from behind a rope | The nose clears the hangar door; the crowd raises their arms and cheers; camera static, wide | 7]

And then Trippe made the biggest bet of his life. He asked Boeing to build a plane more than twice the size of the 707. He ordered twenty-five of them before it existed. In January 1970, Pan Am flew the very first Boeing 747 passenger flight, from New York to London.

SAM (location) {walks along the row of empty seats to the window}: Now watch this. Pan Am didn't just fly the first jumbo jet. It helped will it into existence. It was the most confident company in the world.

[REC 05 | 1970s | An elegant upper-deck lounge on a 1970s jumbo jet, passengers in suits with cocktails on curved sofas, oval windows with evening light, no logos | A passenger lifts a glass toward the window and laughs; the cabin hums; camera static, medium | 6]

## Chapter 3: Two shocks (7:00 to 9:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "What grounded the most famous airline on Earth?"]

[REC 06 | 1970s | A 1973 gas station with a long line of cars and a hand-lettered sign on a pump (blank), drivers standing beside their cars, grey morning | A man checks his watch and leans on his car roof; the line doesn't move; camera static, long lens | 6]

Then came the shocks. In 1973, the price of oil roughly quadrupled. A jumbo jet burns an enormous amount of fuel, and Pan Am had a fleet of them, many flying half empty.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the Airline Deregulation Act, 1978 | Airline Deregulation Act 1978]

The second shock came in 1978. The US government deregulated the airlines. Before that, the government decided who flew where and at what price. Now anyone could fly anywhere and charge anything.

SAM (location) {looks out at the empty apron}: Here's the problem nobody outside the industry noticed. Pan Am flew to the world. But it barely flew inside America.

The government had kept Pan Am off most domestic routes for decades. So people in Denver or Dallas who wanted to fly to Europe started their trip on another airline, and often finished it on that airline too. Pan Am had the oceans and no roads leading to them.

[REC 07 | 1980s | A new low-cost airline check-in counter in a 1980s terminal mobbed with casual travellers with duffel bags, bright flat fluorescent light, no logos | An agent hands over a boarding pass and waves the next person up; camera handheld, wide | 6]

In 1980 it tried to fix that by buying National Airlines, for a high price, after a bidding war. The two airlines never fit together well. And low-cost airlines were starting to pull fares down everywhere.

## Chapter 4: Selling the pieces (9:30 to 12:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "What happens when you sell the family silver?"]

[REC 08 | 1980s | An executive at a large desk signing a document, shown hands-only, a desk globe beside the papers, warm 3200 K lamp, 1980s | The pen finishes a signature; the hand slides the papers across the desk; camera static, close | 5]

To stay alive, Pan Am started selling what it had built. The skyscraper in New York. Its hotel chain. Then, in 1985, the crown jewel, its routes across the Pacific, sold to United. Then its routes to London.

SAM (study) {spreads his hands}: Each sale bought a year or two. But every piece it sold was a piece that used to make money. You can't shrink your way back to greatness.

[GFX: Shrinking route map, Pan Am's network 1980 to 1991]

[ARCHIVE: Lockerbie memorial and news coverage, December 1988 (memorial images only) | Lockerbie memorial Pan Am 103]

And then, on December 21st, 1988, Pan Am Flight 103 was destroyed by a terrorist bomb over Lockerbie, Scotland. Two hundred and seventy people were killed, in the air and on the ground.

[REC 09 | 1980s | A small memorial of candles and flowers on a stone wall in a Scottish village at dusk, grey winter light, no people in frame | Candle flames move in a light wind; camera static, close | 6]

It was, first, a human tragedy, and the families have spent decades seeking answers. For the airline, it was also a blow it never recovered from. Bookings fell. Security failures came to light. Lawsuits followed.

## Chapter 5: The last flight (12:00 to 14:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "What happened on Pan Am's last day?"]

[REC 10 | 1990s | An airport departures board with rows of mechanical split-flap letters flipping (letters blank or blurred), a few travellers looking up, 1991 | The flaps clatter and settle; a traveller sighs and turns away; camera static | 5]

In 1990, the Gulf War sent fuel prices soaring again, and people stopped flying overseas. In January 1991, Pan Am filed for bankruptcy. It sold most of what was left, the shuttle and the European routes, to Delta. The plan was that a smaller Pan Am, based in Miami, would keep flying to Latin America.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of Pan Am ceasing operations, December 4, 1991 | Pan Am last flight December 1991]

Then, on December 4th, 1991, the money stopped. Pan Am ceased operations that morning.

[REC 11 | 1990s | A wide-body jet landing at dusk at a Miami airport, a small group of airline employees in uniforms standing on the tarmac watching | The jet's wheels touch down with a puff of smoke; one employee raises a hand; camera static, long lens | 7]

The last Pan Am flight landed in Miami that evening. The captain, the story goes, circled the airport once before landing.

SAM (boardroom) {spreads his hands on the table}: When the map was torn up, it had nothing underneath. No home market. No loyal flyers at home. Just the oceans.
