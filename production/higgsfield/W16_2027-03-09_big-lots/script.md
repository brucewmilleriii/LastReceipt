## Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)

[REC 01 | 1980s | A 1980s closeout store with tall bins overflowing with mismatched goods, a woman digging into one and pulling out a boxed blender with a delighted face, flat fluorescent light, no logos | She holds the box up to show her friend, who laughs; camera handheld at bin height | 6]

For most of its life, Big Lots sold leftovers. Other companies' mistakes. The toaster that didn't sell, the cereal in last year's box, the lamp a department store over-ordered. You never knew what you'd find. That was the fun.

Then Big Lots decided to stop being a leftover store. And in 2024, with about fourteen hundred stores, it went bankrupt.

## Chapter 1: Other people's mistakes (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How do you build a store out of leftovers?"]

[ARCHIVE: Sol Shenk photos; Consolidated Stores and Odd Lots stores, 1970s to 1980s (Columbus Dispatch archive, check license) | Sol Shenk; Odd Lots store 1980s]

Big Lots started in 1967 in Columbus, Ohio. A man named Sol Shenk ran a company that bought up closeouts. When a manufacturer made too much of something, or changed a package, or a store went out of business, Shenk would buy the whole lot, cheap. Then he'd sell it, still cheap, but for more than he paid.

Shenk was legendary at it. He'd turn up at a bankrupt company and offer cash for everything in the warehouse, the good stuff and the junk together, as long as he could take it all. Sellers liked him because he was fast and he paid. The story told around the company is that he once bought up hundreds of left-over cars from a famous sports car maker that had gone bust. Whatever was out there, if it was cheap enough, Sol Shenk would find a shelf for it.

[REC 02 | 1970s | A cavernous 1970s warehouse stacked with pallets of mismatched boxed goods, a buyer in a tie walking the aisles with a clipboard, counting | He stops at a pallet, lifts a flap, writes on the clipboard; camera static, wide | 6]

In the eighties those stores became Odd Lots, and then Big Lots. The model was simple. The buyers were the stars. A great buyer could find a truckload of name-brand stuff at a fraction of the price, and the shelves changed every week.

SAM (study) {taps the desk}: Here's the thing about a closeout store. You don't go for something. You go to see what's there. It's a treasure hunt. Remember that word. It comes back.

[ARCHIVE: Closeout warehouse and pallet photos, 1980s to 1990s | closeout merchandise warehouse 1980s]

Picture a buyer's week. On Monday, a call comes in: a candle company changed its labels and has forty thousand candles in the old packaging. On Tuesday, a department store chain closes in the Midwest and its warehouse needs emptying. On Wednesday, a snack maker over-produced a flavor nobody liked. The buyer haggles, makes an offer for the lot, and three weeks later those candles, chairs and crackers are on shelves in hundreds of towns. Nobody planned it. That was the point, and it was also the risk.

## Chapter 2: The good years (3:30 to 6:00)

[REC 03 | 1990s | A busy 1990s discount store with eclectic stacked merchandise, a family pushing a cart with a garden gnome, a box fan and a pack of paper towels, bright fluorescent light, no logos | The kid adds a bag of candy to the cart and the mother pretends not to see; camera handheld, medium | 6]

For years, it worked. Big Lots spread across America, mostly in strip malls in smaller towns and suburbs. It was the place where you got paper towels, a patio chair and a weird snack you'd never seen, all for under forty dollars.

And it was good at a kind of customer the big chains often ignored. Retirees on fixed incomes. Young families stretching a paycheck. People in small towns where the nearest mall was an hour away. For them, Big Lots wasn't a novelty. It was where you stocked up, and where you sometimes found something that made your week.

[ARCHIVE: KB Toys stores, 1990s | KB Toys store 1990s]

The company got ambitious too. In the nineties it bought a big chain of toy stores, KB Toys, and for a while it was one of the largest toy sellers in America. That didn't last. A few years later it sold the toy stores and went back to being one thing, under one name. In 2001, the company itself was renamed Big Lots.

But there was a problem built into the model. The supply of good leftovers isn't steady. Some years there are lots. Some years manufacturers get better at not making too much, and the treasure dries up.

And manufacturers were getting much better at it. Computers and better forecasting meant factories made closer to what they could sell. Big retailers like Walmart squeezed their suppliers to plan carefully. And when there were leftovers, the internet gave manufacturers new places to dump them. The pile of other people's mistakes, the thing Big Lots was built on, was quietly getting smaller.

SAM (study) {spreads his hands on the desk}: Imagine running a restaurant where you only cook what the farmers couldn't sell. Great when there's plenty. Not so great when the farmers get better at farming.

[GFX: Line chart, US closeout supply vs Big Lots store count, 1990 to 2015 (illustrative, label as such until sourced)]

And then other stores got into the same game. Dollar stores. Off-price chains like TJ Maxx and Ross, which had huge buying teams and better locations. Ollie's, which opened stores that looked a lot like the old Big Lots.

[ARCHIVE: Ollie's Bargain Outlet storefronts, 2010s | Ollie's Bargain Outlet store]

Ollie's is the interesting one. It started in Pennsylvania in the eighties with a cartoon logo, hand-drawn signs and a slogan, good stuff cheap. It did exactly what Big Lots used to do. It bought closeouts, it changed its shelves every week, and it made the hunt fun. While Big Lots was moving away from the treasure hunt, Ollie's was building a business on it.

## Chapter 3: Becoming a furniture store (6:00 to 9:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Big Lots stop selling closeouts?"]

[ARCHIVE: Big Lots furniture and "Lots to Love" ads, 2010s | Big Lots furniture ad]

So in the 2010s, Big Lots made a decision. Less treasure hunt, more of a steady home store. It leaned hard into furniture, home decor and seasonal goods. Sofas, mattresses, patio sets. It bought a furniture brand. It made its own products instead of hunting for other people's.

There were good reasons for it. Furniture was one thing Big Lots had always been decent at, and the profit on a sofa is a lot bigger than on a box of crackers. A store full of planned products is easier to run than a store waiting to see what turns up. You can advertise a sofa. You can't really advertise a mystery.

[REC 04 | 2010s | A discount store furniture section with rows of sofas and recliners under bright light, a couple in their thirties testing a sofa, a salesperson standing nearby | The couple sit down together and look at each other; camera static, wide | 6]

SAM (location) {rests a hand on a plastic-wrapped sofa}: Now, on paper this makes sense. A sofa is a big sale. People buy furniture every year. You can plan it.

SAM (location) {pats the plastic}: But here's the catch. A sofa isn't a treasure hunt. You don't find one by accident. And there's already a long list of stores that sell sofas.

[REC 05 | 2020s | A suburban living room in 2020, a family on a new sofa watching TV, boxes and packing plastic still on the floor, warm lamp light | A kid bounces on the cushion; the father pulls packing plastic off the arm; camera static, wide | 6]

Then the pandemic made it look brilliant. In 2020, everybody was stuck at home with stimulus money. People bought furniture like never before. Big Lots had a record year.

[GFX: Big number, "2020: record year"]

Think about what that year did to the people running the company. Every chart said the furniture plan was working. Every meeting must have felt like a victory lap. And that's exactly when a company is least likely to ask whether the plan is right.

## Chapter 4: The hangover (9:00 to 12:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "What happened when the stimulus money ran out?"]

[GFX: Bar chart, Big Lots sales and profit 2019 to 2023: a spike in 2020, then losses]

But a sofa you bought in 2020 is a sofa you don't need in 2022. As prices rose across the country, Big Lots' customers, many of them on tight budgets, stopped buying big things first. Furniture sales fell. And the closeout shoppers who used to come for the hunt had found other stores.

[REC 06 | 2020s | A discount store at 2 pm on a weekday with an almost empty parking lot and two cars, grey sky, the store sign blank | A shopping cart sits alone in a cart corral; a car pulls out and leaves; camera static, wide | 6]

Big Lots started losing money. To raise cash, it sold some of its own buildings and distribution centers and rented them back. That's money now, and rent forever.

[GFX: Simple diagram: "Own the warehouse: no rent" becomes "Sell the warehouse: cash once, rent every month"]

This move has a name, a sale-leaseback, and we've seen it before on this channel, at Sears. It's not always a mistake. Sometimes it buys the time a company needs to fix itself. But it only works if the time is used well. If the business underneath doesn't get better, you've just added a new monthly bill to a company that was already struggling to pay its old ones.

Then inflation hit the things Big Lots' customers needed every week. Groceries, gas, rent. When a family's budget gets tight, the first thing to go is the big purchase you can put off. The second is the trip that might turn up something fun. Big Lots had moved its whole business toward the first, and away from being the cheapest place for the basics.

SAM (location) {looks around the dark floor}: Now watch this. Big Lots was squeezed from both sides. Too expensive and boring for the bargain hunters. Not good enough for the furniture shoppers.

[REC 07 | 2020s | An employee stacking unsold patio chairs in the back stockroom of a discount store, rows of boxed furniture to the ceiling, fluorescent light | She lifts one more chair onto the stack and looks up at the tower; camera static, medium | 6]

## Chapter 5: The fall (12:00 to 14:00)

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the Big Lots Chapter 11 filing, September 2024, and closing sales | Big Lots bankruptcy 2024]

In September 2024, Big Lots filed for bankruptcy. It planned to sell itself to a buyer and keep most stores open. That deal fell apart in December. For a few weeks, it looked like every store would close.

Imagine working in one of those stores that December. You'd been told in the fall that a buyer was coming and your job was probably safe. Then, right before Christmas, the deal was off, and the stores started hanging going-out-of-business banners. Shoppers came in looking for bargains on a company that had run out of them. Some longtime staff said the hardest part wasn't losing the job. It was watching regulars, people they'd known for years, pick through the last shelves.

Then, at the last minute, a liquidation firm teamed up with a regional discount chain to save part of it.

[REC 08 | 2020s | A worker on a ladder hanging a large blank closing-sale banner across the front of a discount store at dusk, shoppers walking in underneath | The banner drops into place; a shopper glances up; camera static, wide | 6]

[REC 09 | 2020s | Shoppers in a discount store pushing carts past half-empty shelves with yellow clearance tags (blank), one woman examining a lamp, harsh fluorescent light | She puts the lamp in her cart and keeps going; camera handheld | 6]

In the end, a few hundred stores were bought and reopened under the Big Lots name by another discount company. Hundreds of others closed for good. Thousands of people lost their jobs.

And in a final twist, some of the empty Big Lots buildings were taken over by its old rival. Ollie's moved into dozens of them. The treasure hunt came back to those strip malls. It just had a different name on the sign.

## The verdict (14:00 to 15:30)

[GFX: Timeline recap: 1967 closeouts in Columbus, 1980s Odd Lots, 2001 renamed Big Lots, 2010s furniture push, 2020 record year, 2022 losses, 2024 bankruptcy]

Let's go back over it. A man who bought other people's mistakes by the truckload. Stores where every visit was a little treasure hunt. A supply of leftovers that slowly dried up. A turn toward sofas that the pandemic made look like genius. And then the hangover, landing on customers who had less to spend than anyone.

SAM (boardroom) {stands at the head of the table, the mismatched teacup on the table}: So what killed Big Lots? Inflation, the pandemic hangover, Ollie's, TJ Maxx. All of that.
