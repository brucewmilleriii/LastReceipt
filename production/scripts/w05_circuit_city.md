## Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)

[REC 01 | 2000s | A row of employee name tags on a glass electronics counter, and a manager's hand sweeping them into a cardboard box, store fluorescent light 4200 K | The hand sweeps the tags into the box one by one; camera static, close | 5]

On a Wednesday morning in March 2007, about thirty-four hundred people who worked for Circuit City were told they no longer had a job.

They hadn't done anything wrong. That was the point. They were the best paid salespeople in their stores. The ones who'd been there longest, who knew the products, who had the regular customers. The company said they were being paid more than the market rate.

And they'd be replaced, it said, with people who'd do the job for less.

## Chapter 1: The TV shop (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did one TV shop become a giant?"]

[REC 02 | 1950s | A small 1949 radio and television shop on a Richmond, Virginia street, a man in shirtsleeves standing in the doorway, a few boxy TV sets in the window, bright afternoon sun | He steps out and nods to a passer-by; a car passes; camera static across the street | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Samuel Wurtzel and early Wards stores, 1950s to 1970s | Wards Company Richmond Samuel Wurtzel]

In 1949, a man named Samuel Wurtzel opened a little shop in Richmond, Virginia. He sold one thing that was about to change every living room in America. Televisions.

The story goes that Wurtzel was on vacation in Richmond, getting a haircut, when the barber told him television was coming to the South. He decided to stay. He called the shop Wards, after the first letters of the family's names, and he sold TVs to families who'd never owned one, in a city that had only just started getting a signal.

[REC 03 | 1950s | A family gathered around their first television in a 1950 living room, wide-eyed kids on the rug, parents on the sofa, the screen glow on their faces | The youngest child crawls closer to the screen; the father laughs; camera static, wide | 6]

The business grew with every new gadget. Color TV. Stereos. VCRs. Microwaves. In 1984 it took a new name, Circuit City, and it built huge stores with walls of televisions and something else that mattered a great deal: salespeople who knew what they were talking about.

[REC 04 | 1990s | A busy 1990s electronics superstore with a wall of glowing CRT TVs, a salesman in a shirt and tie explaining a stereo receiver to a young couple, shoppers in the background | The salesman points to a feature; the couple nod and lean in; camera handheld, medium | 6]

They were paid on commission. The more you sold, the more you made. So they learned the products, and they learned you.

If you bought a stereo at Circuit City in 1990, there's a good chance the person who sold it to you could tell you why one speaker sounded warmer than another, and which receiver would still be working in ten years. That kind of person was the real product. The boxes were the same as everywhere else.

[ARCHIVE: Alan Wurtzel photos, 1970s to 1980s | Alan Wurtzel Circuit City]

Samuel's son, Alan Wurtzel, ran the company in its big growth years. He was careful, curious, and willing to bet on new ideas. Under him, the company kept asking the same question every new gadget raised. What do people need to understand before they'll buy this? And then it hired people who could answer.

[ARCHIVE: Cover and pages of Good to Great by Jim Collins (2001), as a book shot | Good to Great Jim Collins]

By the late nineties, Circuit City was the biggest electronics chain in America, and one of the best investments on the stock market. A famous business book, Good to Great, held it up as one of the great American companies. Its stock had beaten the market many times over.

SAM (study) {raises his eyebrows}: Hold onto that. A bestselling book called it great in 2001. Eight years later it didn't exist.

[ARCHIVE: Early CarMax lots, 1990s | CarMax 1993]

And here's something most people don't know. In 1993, Circuit City started a second business. It looked at how miserable it was to buy a used car, the haggling and the tricks, and it built a used car store with fixed prices, big clean lots, and no pressure. It was called CarMax. Circuit City later spun it off as its own company. CarMax is still around today. It's a giant.

SAM (study) {spreads his fingers on the desk}: So the same company that knew how to fix a bad shopping experience for cars forgot how to protect a good one for TVs.

## Chapter 2: The rival (3:30 to 6:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Best Buy win?"]

[ARCHIVE: Best Buy stores in the 1990s; open-floor layout | Best Buy store 1990s]

Circuit City's big rival came from Minnesota. Best Buy. And in the early nineties, Best Buy made a choice that sounded crazy. It got rid of commission. Its salespeople in blue shirts were paid by the hour. Shoppers could browse without anyone hovering.

[REC 05 | 1990s | A big-box electronics store with open aisles and no salespeople nearby, shoppers browsing CDs and video games on their own, bright even fluorescent light | A teenager pulls a CD from a rack and reads the back; camera static, wide | 6]

Circuit City people laughed at that. Their salespeople sold more. But a lot of shoppers liked being left alone. And Best Buy was cheaper.

There's something else that mattered, and it's less exciting. Many of Circuit City's stores were older and smaller, built in places that had been great in the eighties. Best Buy was building bigger stores in newer suburbs, where the young families were moving. Every year the map tilted a little further away from Circuit City.

[REC 06 | 2000s | A last refrigerator being wheeled out of an electronics store on a hand truck to a delivery truck, two workers watching from the loading dock | The fridge rolls up the ramp into the truck; one worker pulls down the door; camera static | 6]

Then Circuit City made its first big mistake. In 2000, it decided to stop selling big appliances. Washers, dryers, refrigerators. They took up space, they were a pain to deliver, and the margins were thin.

SAM (location) {walks along the bare wall of empty TV brackets}: Now watch this. Appliances were boring. But they were the reason a lot of families walked in the door in the first place. You came for the fridge and left with the TV.

Best Buy kept selling appliances. Home Depot and Lowe's grew them. And Circuit City gave away its reason to visit.

It also had a strange detour. In the late nineties, it bet on its own movie format, a pay-per-view disc called DIVX that self-destructed after two days unless you paid more. Customers hated it. It lasted about a year and cost the company a lot of money.

[ARCHIVE: DIVX player and disc photos, 1998 to 1999 | DIVX disc 1998]

The idea behind DIVX was clever on paper. Buy a cheap disc, watch it for two days, and if you want to watch it again, pay a little more through a phone line plugged into the player. But it asked people to pay for something they could already rent, and to plug their DVD player into the phone. The shelves of DVDs in other stores just kept growing.

## Chapter 3: The cut (6:00 to 9:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why fire your best salespeople?"]

[ARCHIVE: Philip Schoonover photos; coverage of the 2007 layoffs | Circuit City layoffs 2007 Schoonover]

By 2006, Circuit City was losing ground fast. Flat-screen TVs were the hottest thing in electronics, and Walmart and Best Buy were winning that fight on price. The new CEO, Philip Schoonover, who had come over from Best Buy, needed to cut costs.

Flat screens were a strange product to fight over. Every year they got bigger and cheaper. A TV that cost a few thousand dollars one Christmas might cost noticeably less the next. That's great for customers. It's brutal for a store, because the profit on each set keeps shrinking while you still have to pay for the floor space and the staff to sell it.

[REC 07 | 2000s | Employees in their thirties to fifties carrying cardboard boxes of personal belongings across a store parking lot at dusk, sodium lights flickering on | One stops and looks back at the store, then keeps walking; camera static, wide | 6]

So in March 2007, Circuit City did it. About thirty-four hundred of its best paid store employees were let go, all at once. They could reapply for their old jobs after ten weeks, at the lower pay.

[GFX: Physical number: "About 8% of store staff. Saved: roughly $110 million a year in wages, by the company's estimate."]

The idea made sense on a spreadsheet. Same number of people on the floor, lower wages, savings straight to the bottom line.

And to be fair, it's worth understanding why it looked smart in a boardroom. Selling electronics was getting harder every year. Prices were falling, the internet was rising, and Wall Street wanted to see costs come down. Wages were one of the biggest costs a store had. And the people paid the most were, by definition, the most expensive line on the sheet. If you only look at the sheet, firing them is the obvious move.

SAM (location) {stops and faces camera}: Here's the thing. The spreadsheet had a column for what those people cost. It didn't have one for what they knew.

[REC 08 | 2000s | A confused middle-aged customer standing alone in a TV aisle of big flat screens, looking around for help, no staff in sight | He looks left, then right, then walks out of frame; camera static, wide | 6]

Customers noticed right away. The person who used to know which TV to buy was gone, and the new person was learning on the job. Stories spread about the layoffs. Shoppers felt it was cruel. And the people who were left, the ones who still had jobs, got the message too: if you get good at this, you get fired.

Picture the store the week after. A manager on the floor with half the experience he had last month. A new hire in a red shirt trying to explain the difference between plasma and LCD, from a card. A customer who used to ask for Dave by name, and doesn't see Dave. You don't need a business degree to know what that customer does next. He drives to the other store.

SAM (study) {leans in}: This is the part people miss. Companies rarely die from one bad quarter. They die when the people they depend on stop believing in them. Customers, and staff.

## Chapter 4: The crisis (9:30 to 12:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Could anyone have saved Circuit City?"]

[GFX: Line chart, Circuit City share price, 2006 to 2008]

Sales kept falling. The stock collapsed.

Inside the company, the plans kept coming. New store designs. A new focus on services, like home installation. Smaller stores. Each one was announced as the fix. None of them could replace what had walked out the door. And the best people who hadn't been fired started quietly taking jobs at Best Buy, which was happy to have them.

In 2008, a surprising buyer stepped forward with an offer to buy the whole company. Blockbuster. Yes, that Blockbuster, from our first episode, run at the time by Jim Keyes, who wanted to turn his stores into entertainment shops.

SAM (study) {dry smile}: One dying chain trying to save itself by buying another dying chain. That deal fell apart in a few months, which may be the luckiest thing that ever happened to Blockbuster's shareholders.

[REC 09 | 2000s | A 2008 trading floor at night, a big screen of red falling numbers above empty desks, one trader rubbing his eyes | The trader sits back and exhales; screens flicker; camera static | 5]

Then came the financial crisis. People stopped buying TVs. Banks stopped lending. And electronics makers, worried they wouldn't get paid, stopped shipping Circuit City products on credit right before the holidays.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of Circuit City's Chapter 11 filing, November 2008 | Circuit City bankruptcy November 2008]

On November 10th, 2008, Circuit City filed for bankruptcy. It hoped to find a buyer. Nobody came.

That Christmas was the worst possible time to be selling TVs with no money. Suppliers wanted to be paid in cash, up front. Shelves had gaps. And shoppers who heard the word bankruptcy on the news wondered whether their warranty would still be good. They bought their TVs somewhere else.

## Chapter 5: Liquidation (12:30 to 14:30)

[REC 10 | 2000s | Workers on ladders hanging large blank banners in an electronics store, shoppers below already picking through discounted stock | A banner unrolls down the wall; a shopper looks up; camera handheld | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Circuit City liquidation sale photos, January to March 2009 | Circuit City liquidation 2009]

In January 2009, the company announced it would close every store. About five hundred and sixty of them, and about thirty-four thousand jobs. Ten times the number of people it had fired to save money.

[REC 11 | 2000s | An empty electronics store with bare walls where TVs used to hang, outlet covers and dust outlines left behind, one fluorescent row on | Dust drifts in the light; camera slowly dollies forward along the wall | 6]

By March, the last stores were dark. Best Buy, its rival, picked up many of its customers, and for a while became the last big electronics chain standing.

The name lived on for a while, bought by another company and used for a website. But the stores, and the people who knew what was inside the boxes, were gone for good.

[GFX: Side by side: "Fired to save money, March 2007: about 3,400 jobs." "Lost when the stores closed, 2009: about 34,000 jobs."]

Put those two numbers next to each other. About thirty-four hundred people were let go to save the company money. Two years later, about thirty-four thousand lost their jobs when the company ran out of money. The cut didn't save the jobs. It was the first crack in all of them.

[REC 12 | 2010s | A former big-box storefront being bricked over by two masons, an outline of a plug-shaped entrance (shape only, no logo), overcast | A mason lays a brick and taps it level; camera static, medium | 6]

## The verdict (14:30 to 16:00)

[GFX: Timeline recap: 1949 TV shop, 1984 Circuit City name, 1993 CarMax, 2000 quits appliances, 2001 "Good to Great", 2007 layoffs, 2008 bankruptcy, 2009 closed]

Let's go back over it. A TV shop that grew by hiring people who understood the products. A business book that called it great. A rival that let shoppers browse in peace. A choice to walk away from appliances. And then, under pressure, a decision to fire the very people who had made the stores worth visiting.

SAM (boardroom) {stands at the head of the table}: So what killed Circuit City? Not Best Buy. Not the recession. Those just finished the job.

SAM (boardroom) {taps the table once}: It cut the one thing customers actually came for. People who knew what they were talking about.
