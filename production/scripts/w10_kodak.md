## Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)

[REC 01 | 1970s | A 1975 engineering lab, a young man in a short-sleeve shirt and tie soldering a boxy prototype on a workbench, oscilloscopes and wire spools around him, flat fluorescent light | Solder smoke rises; he leans in and adjusts a wire with tweezers; camera static, medium | 6]

In 1975, a twenty-four-year-old engineer named Steve Sasson built something strange in a lab in Rochester, New York. It was about the size of a toaster and weighed around eight pounds. It took twenty-three seconds to save a single black-and-white picture onto a cassette tape.

It was the world's first digital camera. And he built it at Kodak.

[REC 02 | 1970s | Close-up of a toaster-sized camera prototype with exposed circuit boards, a lens bolted to the front and a cassette deck on the side, on a lab bench | A hand presses a button; the cassette reels start turning; camera static, macro | 5]

SAM (study) {holds a small yellow film box}: When he showed it to his bosses, they understood exactly what it meant. That was the problem. A camera that doesn't need film. And Kodak was the film company.

SAM (study) {sets the box down}: I'm Sam. This is Last Receipt. Tonight, the rise and fall of Kodak, the company that invented its own replacement.

## Chapter 1: You press the button (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did Kodak turn everyone into a photographer?"]

[ARCHIVE: George Eastman portraits; 1888 Kodak camera ads "You press the button, we do the rest"; Brownie camera ads (public domain) | George Eastman; Kodak 1888 advertisement; Brownie camera 1900]

Before Kodak, photography was for professionals with glass plates and chemicals. In 1888, a former bank clerk named George Eastman sold a simple box camera loaded with enough film for a hundred pictures. When you finished, you mailed the whole camera back. Kodak developed the pictures, reloaded it, and sent it home. The slogan was: you press the button, we do the rest.

In 1900 came the Brownie. It cost one dollar.

[REC 03 | 1950s | A family at the beach in the 1950s, the father holding a small box camera to his eye while the kids squint into the sun by a sandcastle, bright golden light | The father clicks the shutter; the kids break their pose and laugh; camera static, wide | 6]

And here's how Kodak really made its money. Not on the cameras. On the film. Every picture you took meant film you had to buy, and prints you had to pay for. Cameras were the razor. Film was the blade.

[REC 04 | 1980s | Rolls of yellow film boxes moving along a factory conveyor in an endless line, 1980s manufacturing plant, flat fluorescent light, workers in white coats at the far end, no logos | The boxes stream past toward camera; a worker lifts one and checks it; camera static, low | 6]

By the 1970s, Kodak sold about nine out of every ten rolls of film bought in America. "A Kodak moment" became part of the language. The company employed well over a hundred thousand people, many of them in Rochester, where Kodak was simply the town.

SAM (study) {taps the film box}: Here's the thing. Film was one of the most profitable products in the history of American business. And when you have a product like that, every new idea gets one question. Will it hurt the film?

## Chapter 2: Don't tell anyone (3:30 to 6:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Kodak hide the digital camera?"]

[ARCHIVE: Steve Sasson with his 1975 prototype (Kodak press images, check license) | Steve Sasson digital camera 1975]

[REC 05 | 1970s | A 1970s conference room, executives in suits watching a demonstration, arms crossed, seen as silhouettes against a projection screen | One executive leans to another and whispers; the others keep watching; camera static, wide from behind | 6]

Sasson showed his camera around Kodak. Years later he described the reaction. The executives asked why anyone would want to look at pictures on a TV. Where would you keep them? He remembered one summary of the response: that's cute, but don't tell anyone about it.

[REC 06 | 1970s | An old TV screen on a lab cart slowly displaying a blurry black-and-white image line by line, three men watching in a darkened lab | The picture fills in line by line; one man leans closer; camera static over their shoulders | 6]

Kodak did patent it. And in 1981, when Sony showed off an electronic camera of its own, Kodak commissioned a big internal study on digital. It came back with a clear message. Digital would replace film. But it would take about ten years. Kodak had a decade to get ready.

SAM (location) {lifts a strip of negatives up into the red light}: Now watch this. Kodak didn't ignore that report. It used the ten years to protect film for as long as possible.

## Chapter 3: Kodak's other secret (6:30 to 9:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Did Kodak build Instagram first?"]

To be fair, Kodak did go digital. Eventually.

[ARCHIVE: Kodak EasyShare digital cameras and ads, 2003 to 2007 | Kodak EasyShare camera]

By 2005 Kodak was selling more digital cameras in America than anyone. Its EasyShare cameras were everywhere. But the profit on a digital camera was tiny next to film, and the Japanese electronics companies were better at making them. Kodak was winning sales, and losing money on many of them.

[ARCHIVE: Ofoto and Kodak Gallery website screenshots, 2001 to 2011 (Wayback Machine) | Ofoto Kodak Gallery]

And here's the part almost nobody remembers. In 2001, Kodak bought a photo-sharing website called Ofoto. You could upload your digital pictures, share them with family, and keep them online. Years before Facebook.

SAM (location) {hangs the negative strip on the line with a peg}: Kodak owned a photo-sharing website before Facebook existed. And what did it use it for?

SAM (location) {turns to camera}: To get people to order prints. Because prints meant paper and chemicals. It was still protecting the film business.

[REC 07 | 1990s | A busy 1990s photo-processing counter in a drugstore, envelopes of prints in bins, a teenager flipping through a fresh pack of photos and laughing | She flips to the next photo and shows her friend; camera handheld, medium | 6]

The site became Kodak Gallery. It kept nudging people to print. And people were starting to want something else: to share pictures, not hold them.

## Chapter 4: The cliff (9:30 to 12:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "What happens when the film stops?"]

[GFX: Bar chart, Kodak revenue and employees, 1996 to 2011]

Film sales didn't fade slowly. They fell off a cliff. Then phones started taking pictures, and the camera itself started to disappear.

[REC 08 | 2000s | A teenager in 2008 taking a selfie with a flip phone at a party, the flash firing, friends crowding into frame | The flash pops; they crowd around the tiny screen to look; camera handheld | 5]

[ARCHIVE: Kodak Park demolitions in Rochester, 2000s (news photos) | Kodak Park demolition Rochester]

[REC 09 | 2000s | A giant brick industrial building being demolished by controlled implosion on a grey winter day, dust cloud rolling outward, crowd watching from behind a fence | The building drops and the dust cloud rolls toward the crowd; camera static, wide | 7]

Kodak cut tens of thousands of jobs. In Rochester, it blew up old factory buildings it no longer needed, and people came out to watch. It spent billions trying to break into printers. It sued Apple and others over its digital camera patents, which had brought in billions of dollars in licensing fees.

[REC 10 | 2010s | A dusty photo lab with large processing machines covered in plastic sheets, a forgotten calendar on the wall, cold daylight through high windows | A sheet shifts in a draft; dust floats in the light; camera slowly pushes along the row | 6]

In January 2012, Kodak filed for bankruptcy.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of Facebook buying Instagram, April 2012 | Facebook acquires Instagram April 2012]

And less than three months later, Facebook bought a photo-sharing app called Instagram. It had thirteen employees. The price was one billion dollars.

SAM (study) {quietly}: And Kodak Gallery, its own photo site? Sold that same year to a rival, for about twenty-four million.

## Chapter 5: After (12:30 to 13:30)

[REC 11 | 2010s | A smartphone screen scrolling endlessly through photos (blurred), held in two hands on a train, 2012 | The thumb flicks and the photos blur past; camera static, close | 5]

Kodak came out of bankruptcy in 2013 as a much smaller company, making commercial printing and chemicals. The film business still exists, and lately young people have started buying it again. But Kodak will never again be the company that had nine out of ten rolls in America.

[REC 12 | modern | A shoebox of old printed photographs being opened in a dusty attic, a beam of light catching floating dust, a hand lifting out one faded print | The hand lifts the print into the light; camera static, close | 6]

SAM (boardroom) {sets the box on the table}: Kodak saw the future clearly, and spent thirty years trying to slow it down, because the present was so profitable.
