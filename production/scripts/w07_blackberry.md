## Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)

[REC 01 | 2000s | A businessman in a 2005 airport lounge typing on a keyboard phone with both thumbs, coat still on, rolling bag beside him, cool 4200 K terminal light | His thumbs type fast; he glances at a departure board and keeps typing; camera static, medium | 6]

[ARCHIVE: 2009 news photos of President Obama with his BlackBerry | Obama BlackBerry 2009]

In 2009, the new president of the United States had a problem with his security team. They wanted to take away his phone. He fought them, and he won. He was allowed to keep it, a specially secured version. It was a BlackBerry.

People called it the CrackBerry. You couldn't put it down. At its peak, about half the smartphones sold in America were BlackBerrys.

## Chapter 1: Waterloo (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did a small Canadian company beat Silicon Valley?"]

[REC 02 | 1990s | A snowy small-city university campus in Ontario at night in 1999, brick buildings with lit windows, footprints in fresh snow | Snow falls; a student crosses the quad with a backpack; camera static, wide | 6]

BlackBerry didn't come from California. It came from Waterloo, Ontario, a cold college town about an hour west of Toronto.

[ARCHIVE: Mike Lazaridis and Jim Balsillie photos, 1990s to 2000s | Mike Lazaridis; Jim Balsillie]

In 1984, an engineering student named Mike Lazaridis started a company there with a friend. It was called Research In Motion, RIM for short. For years they did contract work on wireless gadgets. Then, in the early nineties, a sharp business guy named Jim Balsillie joined, put his own money in, and became co-CEO. Lazaridis built things. Balsillie sold them.

Those early years were scrappy. Lazaridis had left university just short of finishing his degree to start the company. RIM made whatever it could get paid for: signs, network gear, even a barcode reader for movie film that helped editors keep sound and pictures in sync, which won the company awards from the film and TV world. None of it was famous. All of it taught a small team in a cold town how to make radios and software that didn't break.

[ARCHIVE: University of Waterloo campus, 1980s; RIM's early offices | University of Waterloo 1980s]

And they got very good at one thing: squeezing data through slow wireless networks without draining a battery. That sounds dull. It was the whole secret.

[REC 03 | 1990s | Engineers in a cramped late-1990s office testing a pager-like device with a tiny keyboard, circuit boards and pizza boxes on the desk, CRT monitors | One engineer types on the tiny keyboard and holds it up to show the other; camera handheld, close | 6]

In 1999 they launched a little device that clipped to your belt. It had a tiny keyboard and did one thing perfectly. It pushed your work email to you, instantly, wherever you were. Not when you dialed in. The moment it arrived.

[ARCHIVE: Early BlackBerry 850 and 957 device photos, 1999 to 2000 | BlackBerry 850 1999]

The name came from a branding firm. Someone looked at the rows of tiny round keys and thought they looked like the seeds on a berry. Strawberry was considered. BlackBerry won. It sounded friendly, which mattered, because this was a work gadget, and work gadgets were usually grey and dull.

SAM (study) {taps the desk with two thumbs}: Here's the thing. Busy people didn't want a computer in their pocket. They wanted their inbox in their pocket. And BlackBerry gave them exactly that.

## Chapter 2: CrackBerry (3:30 to 6:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why couldn't anyone put it down?"]

[REC 04 | 2000s | A crowded commuter train in 2006 where almost every passenger in suits is thumb-typing on a keyboard phone, morning light through the windows | Everyone types; one man smiles at his screen; the train sways; camera handheld from a seat | 6]

By the mid-2000s, the BlackBerry was a status symbol. Bankers, lawyers, senators, celebrities. The keyboard was fast and satisfying. The battery lasted for days. And the security was so good that governments trusted it.

Its reputation for working when nothing else did grew from stories like September 11th, 2001, when phone lines in New York and Washington were jammed and some people found their BlackBerry messages still getting through. In Washington, that kind of story spreads fast. Soon half of Congress was thumbing away in meetings.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the NTP v. RIM patent case, 2005 to 2006 | NTP RIM patent settlement 2006]

There was one scare. A small patent company sued RIM, claiming it owned the idea behind wireless email. The fight went on for years, and at one point it looked like a judge might shut BlackBerry service down across America. In 2006, RIM paid more than six hundred million dollars to make it go away. It could afford it. That's how big it had become.

[REC 05 | 2000s | A family dinner table in 2007, a father in a work shirt checking a keyboard phone under the table while his wife and two kids look at him, warm 2800 K pendant light | The wife puts down her fork and stares; he slips the phone into his pocket; camera static, wide | 6]

You probably remember the little red light. It blinked in the corner whenever a new message came in. In a meeting, at dinner, on a beach, people would glance at it every few minutes, like a nervous habit. And at the bottom of millions of emails was a little line that said "Sent from my BlackBerry". It was an apology for typos, and a brag at the same time.

It was so addictive that the word CrackBerry was named a word of the year by an American dictionary. Families complained about it at dinner tables. Then BlackBerry added one more thing.

[ARCHIVE: BlackBerry Messenger (BBM) screens and ads, 2005 to 2012 | BlackBerry Messenger BBM]

It was called BlackBerry Messenger. BBM. Free, instant messages between BlackBerry users, with little delivered and read receipts. A "D" when it arrived, an "R" when they'd read it. No texting fees. Teenagers who couldn't afford a texting plan begged their parents for a BlackBerry just for BBM.

Remember BBM. It comes back.

[GFX: Line chart, BlackBerry subscribers and RIM market value, 2003 to 2008]

By 2008, RIM was one of the most valuable companies in Canada.

At its peak, the company was worth around eighty billion US dollars. A college town in Ontario had built one of the most important tech companies on Earth. The founders were heroes at home. They gave money to universities and research institutes. Waterloo had a nickname, the Silicon Valley of the North, and BlackBerry was the reason.

SAM (study) {leans back, unhurried}: Hold that picture for a moment. Eighty billion dollars, half the smartphones in America, and a messaging app teenagers begged for. Now let's see how fast it goes.

## Chapter 3: No keyboard (6:30 to 9:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why weren't they worried about the iPhone?"]

[ARCHIVE: Steve Jobs introducing the iPhone, January 2007 (still images) | iPhone introduction 2007]

[REC 06 | 2000s | A 2007 office at night, an executive in a shirt watching a product keynote on a laptop, arms crossed, seen in silhouette against the screen glow | He leans back and uncrosses his arms, then crosses them again; camera static from behind | 6]

In January 2007, Apple announced a phone with no keyboard at all. Just glass.

The people at RIM looked at it, and by many accounts, they weren't worried. It had a bad battery. It typed poorly. Its network connection was slow. It couldn't do the secure email that big companies needed.

In the book Losing the Signal, the reporters who covered RIM describe engineers opening up an iPhone and struggling to believe what Apple had done. To them, it looked like it shouldn't work. A phone like that would flood the networks with data and drain its battery by lunch. And in a way they were right. The early iPhone did have a weak battery and a slow connection. They just didn't see that people would put up with that, for a screen that felt like the future.

SAM (location) {walks between the empty desks}: Now watch this. They weren't stupid. They were right about what their customers wanted.

SAM (location) {stops and turns}: They were wrong about who their customers would be.

The BlackBerry was built for the person whose company bought them a phone. The iPhone was built for the person who bought their own. And there were a lot more of them.

[REC 07 | 2000s | A line of excited people outside a phone store at dawn in 2008, wrapped in jackets, holding coffee, one taking a photo | The store lights come on and the line cheers; camera handheld, wide | 6]

[REC 08 | 2000s | Close-up of a hand swiping a glossy touchscreen phone for the first time, the person's face out of focus behind, delighted | The thumb swipes and the hand pulls back in surprise; camera static, macro | 4]

Then came the App Store, and Android. Phones became computers, and computers need apps. Developers went where the users were going.

[GFX: Bars, apps available, App Store vs BlackBerry App World, 2009 to 2012]

And then a quiet shift happened inside offices. Employees started showing up with their own iPhones, and asking the IT department to connect them to work email. For years, IT said no. Then the bosses got iPhones too, and IT said yes. The one wall that protected BlackBerry, the company email server, fell from the inside.

## Chapter 4: The app they had first (9:00 to 11:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Did BlackBerry invent WhatsApp?"]

Now here's the part that still stings.

[ARCHIVE: WhatsApp early logos and 2014 Facebook acquisition coverage | WhatsApp Facebook acquisition 2014]

[GFX: Side by side, BBM 2005 and WhatsApp 2009: free messages, delivered mark, read mark]

In 2009, two former Yahoo engineers started a little messaging app for the iPhone. Free messages over the internet, a tick when it was delivered, two ticks when it was read. Sound familiar? It was called WhatsApp. In 2014, Facebook bought it for about nineteen billion dollars.

SAM (location) {picks up a keyboard phone from an empty desk}: BlackBerry had that exact thing, with tens of millions of users, years earlier. BBM. All it had to do was let iPhone and Android people use it too.

[REC 09 | 2010s | A 2011 boardroom, two executives arguing across a table in silhouette against a snowy window, one jabbing a finger at a printout | One stands up and walks to the window; the other stays seated; camera static, wide | 6]

There was a fight inside RIM about doing exactly that. According to later reporting, Balsillie wanted to put BBM on rival phones around 2011. The idea was turned down. BBM was the reason people bought a BlackBerry, the thinking went. Why give it away?

By the time BBM finally arrived on iPhone and Android in 2013, WhatsApp had already taken the world.

To be fair to the people who said no, it wasn't a crazy argument. BBM really was a reason people bought BlackBerrys, especially young people, and especially in places like Indonesia, the Middle East and parts of Africa, where BBM stayed hugely popular for years. Letting it loose on other phones meant one less reason to buy the hardware. And RIM was a hardware company. That's how it made its money.

But that's exactly the trap. When a company protects the old way of making money, it can stop seeing the new one standing right in front of it.

SAM (study) {quietly}: They didn't lose the app race. They had already won it. And they kept it locked in a box.

## Chapter 5: Too late (11:30 to 14:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "What happened when BlackBerry finally changed?"]

[ARCHIVE: BlackBerry Storm (2008), PlayBook (2011), BlackBerry 10 launch (2013) product photos | BlackBerry Storm; BlackBerry PlayBook; BlackBerry Z10]

RIM did try. Its first touchscreen phone in 2008, the Storm, was clumsy and buggy.

The Storm was meant to answer the iPhone. Its whole screen clicked down like a button when you pressed it, a clever idea that felt strange in the hand. It was rushed out for a big American carrier in time for Christmas. Lots of them came back. The company that had built its name on things that just worked had shipped something that didn't. Its tablet in 2011 shipped without a built-in email app, from the email company. And in late 2011 a breakdown knocked out BlackBerry service for days across much of the world.

For a company whose whole promise was reliability, that outage hurt more than any bad review. Millions of people who'd stuck with BlackBerry out of trust spent three days staring at a phone that wouldn't send a message. Some of them used those three days to shop for something else.

[GFX: Line chart, BlackBerry US smartphone share, 2009 to 2016, falling toward zero]

In 2012, Lazaridis and Balsillie stepped aside. In 2013, the company renamed itself BlackBerry and launched a brand new operating system. It was good. Reviewers liked it. But the apps, the developers and the people had already moved on.

[ARCHIVE: BlackBerry 10 launch event and Z10 coverage, January 2013 | BlackBerry Z10 launch]

It even bought an ad during the Super Bowl. But a phone is only as good as what you can do with it. You'd buy the new BlackBerry, go looking for an app your friends were using, and it wasn't there. So you'd go back to the store and buy what your friends had.

SAM (study) {turns the old keyboard phone face down on the desk}: That's the cruel math of phones. Nobody wants the one their friends can't message.

[REC 10 | 2010s | An empty corporate campus parking lot in winter, snow falling on a few cars, low office buildings with dark windows | Snow falls; one car pulls out and leaves; camera static, wide | 6]

In 2016 BlackBerry stopped making its own phones and became a software and security company.

For a few years, other companies licensed the name and made BlackBerry-branded phones with keyboards, running Android. Some fans bought them out of loyalty. There was even talk of a new keyboard phone with modern 5G. It never shipped.

The company itself survived, smaller and quieter, selling security software to businesses and governments, and software that runs inside cars. So in one sense BlackBerry is still here. You just won't find it in your pocket. And in January 2022, the old BlackBerry services were switched off. The phones still in drawers around the world couldn't make calls or send messages anymore.

## The verdict (14:00 to 15:30)

[GFX: Timeline recap: 1984 RIM founded, 1999 first BlackBerry, 2005 BBM, 2007 iPhone, 2008 Storm, 2009 WhatsApp, 2011 outage, 2013 BBM on rivals, 2016 phones end, 2022 shut off]

Let's run it back. Two founders in a college town who solved a real problem better than anyone. A device so good people called it a drug. A messaging app years ahead of its time. And then a few years of being right about the wrong customer, while the world moved to the glass phone in everyone's pocket.

SAM (boardroom) {stands at the head of the table, a keyboard phone in hand}: So what killed BlackBerry? Everybody says the iPhone.

SAM (boardroom) {sets the phone on the table}: I think BlackBerry was killed by being right for too long. It won by serving one customer perfectly, and it couldn't imagine any other.

[GFX: Red CLOSED stamp lands on a photo of a BlackBerry Bold. One heavy thud.]

SAM (boardroom) {looks into the lens}: Here's the lesson for your job. Your best customer today is not proof of where the market is going. Sometimes it's the opposite.
