## Chapter 1: The supermarket of toys (0:45 to 4:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did a baby furniture store take over Christmas?"]

[ARCHIVE: Charles Lazarus portraits; early Children's Bargain Town and Toys R Us stores, 1950s to 1960s | Charles Lazarus Toys R Us]

The story starts in 1948 in Washington, D.C., with a young man named Charles Lazarus, just back from the war. He opened a shop selling baby furniture. And he noticed something. People bought a crib once. But they kept coming back for toys.

So he started selling toys. Then more toys. And then he had an idea borrowed from the grocery business: what if a toy store worked like a supermarket? Huge, cheap, self-service, with every toy you could think of stacked to the ceiling.

[REC 02 | 1960s | A newly opened 1960s toy supermarket, wide aisles with shelves stacked to the ceiling with boxed toys, families pushing shopping carts, bright daylight-balanced fluorescent light | A boy runs ahead of his parents' cart down the aisle; camera static, high and wide | 6]

In 1957 he opened the first Toys R Us, with the backwards R, so it looked like a kid had written it. Then came the mascot, Geoffrey the giraffe.

[ARCHIVE: Geoffrey the giraffe in ads and store photos | Geoffrey giraffe Toys R Us]

Here's what made it work. Before Toys R Us, toys were mostly a Christmas business. Department stores filled a toy section in November and emptied it in January. Lazarus stocked everything, all year, and priced it low. Toy companies needed him more than he needed them.

[REC 03 | 1980s | A father in a 1980s parka carrying the last boxed game console off a nearly bare shelf during a holiday rush, other shoppers reaching past him, tungsten and fluorescent mix | His hand closes on the last box and pulls it from the shelf; another shopper sighs; camera handheld, chest height | 5]

By the late 1980s, Toys R Us sold about one in every five toys bought in America. Business writers gave it a nickname. The category killer. It didn't compete with toy shops. It erased them.

SAM (study) {holds up one finger}: Remember that phrase, category killer. Because the next one was coming, and it sold everything.

## Chapter 2: The deal with Amazon (4:00 to 7:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Toys R Us hand its website to Amazon?"]

[ARCHIVE: News coverage of the 1999 Christmas online delivery failures | Toys R Us 1999 online delivery Christmas]

In the late nineties the big threat was Walmart, which passed Toys R Us in toy sales around 1998. And then there was the internet.

[REC 04 | 1990s | A Christmas morning in 1999, a living room with a decorated tree and no presents underneath, a mother looking at a printed order confirmation, a child in pyjamas on the stairs | The mother lowers the paper and looks at the child; camera static, wide | 6]

Toys R Us tried to sell toys online for Christmas 1999, and it went badly. Orders didn't arrive in time. Some families woke up on Christmas morning with nothing under the tree, and the government later fined the company over late deliveries.

So in 2000, Toys R Us made a decision that sounds unbelievable now. It signed a ten-year deal with Amazon. Amazon would run the online store. Toys R Us would be the only toy seller on Amazon, and pay Amazon for the privilege.

[REC 05 | 2000s | Two executives in dark suits shaking hands across a conference table in 2000, shown from the shoulders down, contract folders and pens on the table | The handshake ends and one pulls a contract across the table; camera static, close | 5]

SAM (study) {spreads his hands}: Think about that. It handed its online future to the company that would one day eat it.

It got worse. Amazon soon let other sellers list toys too. Toys R Us sued, said Amazon had broken the deal, and won in 2006. But by then six years were gone, and Toys R Us had to build its own online store, almost from scratch, a decade late.

## Chapter 3: The buyout (7:00 to 10:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Who really bought Toys R Us?"]

[ARCHIVE: 2005 news coverage of the KKR, Bain Capital and Vornado buyout | Toys R Us buyout 2005 KKR Bain]

By 2005 Toys R Us was big, slow and worried. So the board sold the company to three investment firms: KKR, Bain Capital and a real estate company called Vornado. The price was about six point six billion dollars.

Now this is the part that matters, so stay with me.

[REC 06 | 2000s | A thick bound loan agreement landing on a dark mahogany desk beside a fountain pen, 2005 office, warm 3000 K desk lamp | The document lands with a slight bounce and settles; camera static, top-down | 5]

[GFX: Animation, a $6.6B price tag; about $1.3B in cash from the buyers, the rest stacked as loans onto the Toys R Us logo]

The buyers didn't pay with their own money. They put in a slice. Most of the price was borrowed. And the borrowed money wasn't put on the buyers' books. It was put on Toys R Us. The company was bought with a loan, and then handed the bill.

SAM (location) {walks into frame between the bare shelves and stops}: This is called a leveraged buyout. It's like buying a house with a tiny down payment, and then making the house pay its own mortgage.

SAM (location) {looks up at the empty top shelf}: If the house has a great year, you win big. If it has a bad one, the house goes to the bank. You don't.

[GFX: Bar, Toys R Us annual interest payments, about $400 million or more a year, 2006 to 2017]

From then on, Toys R Us had to pay interest on about five billion dollars of debt. Year after year, the interest bill was in the hundreds of millions. In some years it was more than the company spent on its stores.

## Chapter 4: Paying the loan (10:00 to 13:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Where did the money go?"]

[REC 07 | 2010s | A store employee in a red vest mopping a nearly empty toy aisle at closing time, scuffed linoleum, water-stained ceiling tile above, half the lights off | He pushes the mop slowly toward camera, then stops and looks at the stain; camera static | 6]

Here's what a company does when every dollar is already spoken for. It doesn't fix the leaky roof. It doesn't hire more people on the floor. It doesn't rebuild the website. It waits.

And the stores showed it. Through the 2010s, customers complained about messy aisles, empty shelves and long lines. Meanwhile Walmart and Target were cutting toy prices to get parents in the door, and Amazon was delivering toys to the porch.

[REC 08 | 2010s | A stack of unpaid invoices growing on an office desk under a fluorescent tube, a calculator beside it, an office chair empty | More invoices are dropped onto the stack by a hand; camera static, time-lapse feel | 5]

SAM (location) {runs a hand along the bare shelf edge}: Now watch this. Toys R Us was still selling billions of dollars of toys a year. Customers hadn't left. The business wasn't the problem. The payment was the problem.

Twice the owners tried to take the company public again to raise money. Both times it didn't happen. In 2017, a big chunk of the debt was coming due, and nobody was lining up to refinance it.

## Chapter 5: The last Christmas (13:00 to 15:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did it close in a single spring?"]

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the Toys R Us bankruptcy filing, September 2017 | Toys R Us bankruptcy 2017]

In September 2017, just before the holidays, Toys R Us filed for bankruptcy. The plan was to shrink, fix things and come back.

But a bankruptcy filing right before Christmas is a disaster for a toy store. Toy makers got nervous about shipping goods. Shoppers worried about gift cards. The holiday season was terrible.

[REC 09 | 2010s | Liquidation day inside a toy store, shoppers carrying armfuls of plush toys past bare shelves, banners hanging from the ceiling (blank), a long checkout line | A woman lifts two plush bears into her cart; the line shuffles forward; camera handheld | 6]

And in March 2018, the lenders gave up. Toys R Us would liquidate. Every US store, closed. By the end of June, the doors were locked.

[REC 10 | 2010s | A giant plush giraffe left alone on an empty steel shelf in a closed toy store, lights switching off row by row toward camera | The last row of lights clicks off and the giraffe goes dark; camera static | 6]

About thirty thousand people lost their jobs, many with no severance at first. After public pressure, the buyout firms agreed to put money into a fund for the workers, a small fraction of what they'd been paid over the years.
