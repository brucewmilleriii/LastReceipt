In 1962, three discount stores opened their very first locations in America, within a few months of each other. One was in Arkansas. It was called Walmart. One was in Minnesota. It was called Target.

## Chapter 1: The head start (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did a dime store build the biggest discount chain?"]

[ARCHIVE: S.S. Kresge five-and-dime storefronts, 1910s to 1950s | S.S. Kresge store]

Kmart didn't come from nowhere. It came from S.S. Kresge, a chain of five-and-dime stores that started in Detroit in 1899. By the 1950s, Kresge had hundreds of stores on main streets across the country, and they were getting old, along with main street.

[ARCHIVE: Harry Cunningham photos; early Kmart stores, 1962 to 1970 | Harry Cunningham Kmart; Kmart 1960s]

A Kresge executive named Harry Cunningham spent two years studying the new discount stores popping up in the suburbs. He came back with a plan. Big stores, out by the highways, with parking lots and low prices on everything. In 1962 he opened the first Kmart in Garden City, Michigan.

[REC 02 | 1960s | A crew building a new single-story discount store on cleared farmland beside a highway in 1965, steel frame and a huge empty parking lot being paved | A cement truck backs in; workers wave it on; camera static, wide | 6]

And then Kmart did what Kresge knew how to do. It opened stores. Fast. Dozens a year, then more than a hundred a year. By the mid-seventies, Kmart was opening stores at a pace nobody had seen in American retail.

SAM (study) {leans back}: Walmart, in those years, was a handful of stores in small towns in Arkansas. If you'd had to bet in 1970, you'd have bet on Kmart. Everybody did.

## Chapter 2: The blue light (3:30 to 6:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "What made the Blue Light Special so powerful?"]

[REC 03 | 1970s | A flashing blue police-style beacon on a tall pole above a sale table in a 1970s discount store aisle, shoppers turning toward it | The blue light starts spinning; two shoppers turn and walk toward it; camera static, medium | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Kmart "Blue Light Special" photos and TV ads | Kmart Blue Light Special]

And then there was the light. A blue police light on a pole, wheeled to a table somewhere in the store. When it started flashing, a voice came over the speakers. "Attention, Kmart shoppers." And for the next few minutes, whatever was on that table was cheap.

[REC 04 | 1970s | A store employee's hand on a gooseneck PA microphone at a service desk, 1970s, warm 3200 K desk light, a paper list of specials beside it | He presses the talk button and leans toward the microphone; camera static, close | 5]

[REC 05 | 1970s | Families rushing toward a sale table piled with folded towels in a 1970s store, hands reaching in, motion blur on the fastest | A woman grabs a stack of towels and turns away smiling; camera handheld at shoulder height | 5]

People loved it. It turned a trip to a discount store into a little game. It became a phrase in the language. You'll still hear people call a good deal a blue light special.

SAM (study) {taps the desk once}: Here's the thing about the Blue Light Special. It worked. And it taught Kmart that the answer to every problem was another sale.

Remember that. It comes back.

[ARCHIVE: Kmart store exteriors at their peak, 1980s | Kmart 1980s store]

By the 1980s, Kmart was the second biggest retailer in America, behind only Sears. More than two thousand stores. It was so confident that it started buying other businesses. A bookstore chain. A home improvement chain. Drugstores. Later, a big stake in an office supply chain, and a sporting goods chain. It even bought the Borders bookstores.

## Chapter 3: Boring stuff (6:00 to 9:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did Walmart win with trucks and computers?"]

Meanwhile, in Bentonville, Arkansas, Walmart was spending its money on things nobody would put in a commercial.

[REC 06 | 1980s | Rows of semi trucks with blank white trailers leaving a giant distribution center at dawn, long shadows, steam from exhausts | Trucks pull out one after another onto the highway; camera slowly rises above the lot | 7]

[ARCHIVE: Walmart distribution centers and satellite network, 1980s | Walmart distribution center 1980s]

Trucks. Its own fleet. Huge distribution centers, each one built so a store was never more than a day's drive away. Barcode scanners at every register, early. And in the late eighties, its own satellite network, so headquarters could see what every store was selling, every day.

[REC 07 | 1980s | A 1980s warehouse office, a worker's hand on the keyboard of a terminal with a green-text monochrome screen, printouts on a clipboard beside it, flat fluorescent light | He types and the green text scrolls; camera static, close over the shoulder | 5]

SAM (location) {walks into frame down the empty main aisle}: Now, why does that matter? Because the most important thing in a store isn't the price. It's whether the thing you came for is on the shelf.

SAM (location) {stops under the flickering light}: Walmart's computers knew what was running out before it ran out. Kmart's often didn't.

[GFX: Side by side, Walmart vs Kmart: distribution costs as a share of sales, late 1980s]

Walmart could move goods from the factory to the shelf for less than Kmart could. So it could charge less, every day, without a flashing light. And every day it got a little bigger.

[GFX: Line chart, annual sales, Kmart vs Walmart, 1980 to 1995, Walmart crossing about 1990]

Around 1990, Walmart passed Kmart in sales. And the gap only got wider.

[REC 08 | 1990s | An empty shelf in a discount store with one orphaned price tag, a fluorescent tube flickering above, 1990s | The tube flickers twice and steadies; camera static, close | 5]

[REC 09 | 1990s | A shopper in a 1990s windbreaker, seen from behind, looking at an empty shelf, sighing, then walking away down the aisle | She pauses, shakes her head, walks away; camera static, wide | 6]

By the late nineties, people had a word for Kmart stores. Tired. Dim lighting, cluttered aisles, empty spots where the thing you came for should have been. Kmart had sold off most of the businesses it bought. It even brought in Martha Stewart to sell sheets and towels. That actually helped. But it couldn't fix the shelves.

## Chapter 4: The price war (9:30 to 12:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Kmart pick a fight it couldn't win?"]

[ARCHIVE: Chuck Conaway photos; "BlueLight Always" ads, 2001 | Kmart BlueLight Always 2001]

In 2000, Kmart hired a new young CEO named Chuck Conaway. He'd come from the drugstore chain CVS, and he had a bold plan. Kmart would beat Walmart on price. Head to head. He cut prices on tens of thousands of items and called it BlueLight Always.

SAM (location) {half a smile}: Now watch this. Challenging Walmart to a price war is like challenging the guy who owns the gym to a push-up contest.

To pay for the price cuts, Kmart cut back on its advertising, including the weekly paper circulars that its customers had used for decades to plan their trips. So prices were a little lower, but fewer people knew. And Walmart simply matched them. Walmart could afford to. Kmart couldn't.

[GFX: Bar, Kmart losses in 2001]

Meanwhile, the systems Kmart had been putting off for years were still a mess. Stores ran out of things. Warehouses filled up with the wrong things.

[REC 10 | 2000s | A newspaper landing on a snowy suburban doorstep at dawn in January 2002, folded with the front page down | The paper lands and slides a little on the snow; camera static, low | 5]

[ARCHIVE: Newspaper front pages about the Kmart bankruptcy, January 2002 | Kmart bankruptcy January 2002]

On January 22nd, 2002, Kmart filed for bankruptcy. At the time, it was the biggest retail bankruptcy in American history. Hundreds of stores closed in the months that followed. Tens of thousands of people lost their jobs.

## Chapter 5: The long goodbye (12:00 to 14:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Who bought Kmart, and why?"]

Kmart came out of bankruptcy in 2003, much smaller. And the man who ended up in control was a hedge fund manager named Eddie Lampert. If you watched our Sears episode, you know that name. In 2004, Kmart bought Sears, and the two old giants became one company.

[REC 11 | 2010s | A huge red-and-white store sign being lowered by a crane from the front of a big-box building under an overcast sky, a few workers watching | The sign swings slightly as it descends; camera static, wide | 7]

[GFX: Count-down map, Kmart stores 2002 to 2026, dots going dark]

It didn't save either of them. Year after year the store count fell. Fifteen hundred. A thousand. A few hundred. Sears Holdings went bankrupt in 2018, and Kmart went down with it.

[ARCHIVE: Photos of the last remaining Kmart stores | last Kmart store]

Today, Kmart has all but disappeared from the mainland United States. The handful of stores still carrying the name are mostly far from Michigan, where it all began.
