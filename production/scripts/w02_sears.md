## Cold open (0:00 to 0:50)

[ARCHIVE: Sears, Roebuck catalog covers and pages, 1897 to 1908 (public domain) | Sears Roebuck catalog 1897]

[REC 01 | 1890s | A Midwest farm kitchen in 1900, a farm family around an oil lamp at a wooden table turning the pages of a thick mail-order catalog, a child pointing at a page | The child points, the mother turns the page; lamp flame flickers; camera static, medium-wide | 6]

Picture a farm in Iowa in 1900. The nearest real store is a day's ride away, and the man who runs it charges whatever he likes. And on the kitchen table, there's a book. A thousand pages. Watches, shoes, stoves, pianos, medicine, a bicycle. Point at it, mail in the order, and it comes to you on the train.

That book was the Sears catalog. People called it the wish book.

[ARCHIVE: Sears Modern Homes catalog pages, 1910s to 1930s (public domain) | Sears Modern Homes catalog]

And at one point, if you turned to the right page, you could order a whole house.

## Chapter 1: The wish book (0:50 to 4:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "How did a railroad clerk beat every store in America?"]

[REC 02 | 1890s | A small wooden railroad depot on the Minnesota prairie, 1886, a young station agent in shirtsleeves and a waistcoat unpacking a crate of pocket watches on the platform | He lifts a watch from the crate and checks it; wind moves the prairie grass; camera static, wide | 6]

It starts with a box of watches nobody wanted. In 1886, a young railroad station agent in Minnesota named Richard Sears got hold of a shipment of watches that a local jeweler had refused. Sears sold them himself, to other station agents up and down the line, and made a nice profit. So he ordered more.

[ARCHIVE: Portraits of Richard Sears and Alvah Roebuck | Richard Warren Sears; Alvah Curtis Roebuck]

He needed someone who could actually fix watches, so he hired a watchmaker named Alvah Roebuck. By 1893 the company was called Sears, Roebuck and Company, and it had stopped being about watches.

Here's what Sears understood. In 1900, most Americans still lived in the country. Their choice was the general store, with a small selection and high prices. Sears offered them everything, at city prices, with a famous promise: if you don't like it, send it back.

[GFX: Map, rural free delivery routes spreading across the US, 1896 to 1913]

And then the government handed Sears a gift. Free mail delivery to rural homes arrived in the 1890s, and in 1913, parcel post. Suddenly the post office would carry a package right to the farm gate.

[REC 03 | 1900s | A vast early-1900s mail-order warehouse in Chicago, hundreds of workers sorting parcels onto wooden chutes and conveyor belts under skylights | Parcels slide down a chute into a worker's arms; workers move in every direction; camera static, high wide angle | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Sears Chicago West Side mail-order plant photos, 1906 onward | Sears Roebuck Chicago plant 1906]

By 1906 Sears had built a mail-order plant in Chicago so big it was among the largest business buildings in the world. Orders came in by the thousands every day, and a system of chutes, belts and timetables sent them out again. People came from other countries just to watch how it worked.

SAM (study) {makes a small circle with one finger on the desk}: Here's the thing. The genius wasn't the catalog. It was the machine behind the catalog. Order, warehouse, delivery, returns. That's Amazon. In 1906.

[REC 04 | 1920s | Men in flat caps assembling a wooden house frame from numbered lumber on a grassy lot in a 1920s Ohio suburb, a boxcar-delivered stack of lumber beside them | Two men raise a wall frame into place and brace it; camera static, wide | 6]

And then there were the houses. From 1908 to 1940, Sears sold kit homes through the mail. You picked your model, a bungalow or a two-story, and about thirty thousand pieces arrived on a railroad car, every board numbered, with a thick instruction book. Tens of thousands of families built them, and many are still standing. People find Sears part numbers stamped on the beams in their attic.

## Chapter 2: The golden age (4:30 to 7:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Sears build the tallest building on Earth?"]

[ARCHIVE: First Sears retail store, Chicago 1925; Sears department stores 1950s | Sears store 1950s]

By the 1920s, cars changed everything. Farmers could drive to town. So Sears, led by a former army general named Robert Wood, did something risky. It opened stores. Big ones, with parking lots, on the edge of town. Within a few years the stores were selling more than the catalog.

[REC 05 | 1950s | A busy 1950s department store tool aisle, a father and teenage son in Saturday clothes holding a socket wrench set, red pegboard walls of hand tools, no logos | The father turns the wrench set in his hands and nods; the son grins; camera static at waist height | 6]

For the next fifty years, Sears was the store. Craftsman tools with a lifetime guarantee. Kenmore washers. DieHard batteries. Allstate insurance, which Sears started. At its height, Sears was the biggest retailer in America, and by some counts, a big share of all the money Americans spent in stores went through it.

[ARCHIVE: Sears Tower construction and completion, Chicago, 1970 to 1973 | Sears Tower construction 1973]

[REC 06 | 1970s | A skyscraper's black steel frame rising over Chicago at sunset in 1972, tower cranes silhouetted on top | A crane boom swings slowly; city traffic below; camera static on a long lens | 6]

In 1973 it finished a new headquarters in Chicago. A hundred and ten stories. For twenty-five years, the Sears Tower was the tallest building in the world.

SAM (study) {leans back in the chair}: That building is the high point of this story. And if you know anything about these stories, you know what usually happens to companies right after they build a monument to themselves.

## Chapter 3: The crack (7:30 to 10:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Who was quietly beating Sears?"]

[ARCHIVE: Early Walmart and Kmart discount stores, 1970s to 1980s | Walmart store 1980s]

While Sears was building its tower, two discount chains were growing in places Sears didn't take seriously. Kmart, in the suburbs. And Walmart, in small towns in Arkansas and Missouri. They sold the same kinds of things, cheaper, and they kept their prices low every day instead of waiting for a sale.

[GFX: Line chart, US retail sales, Sears vs Walmart, 1980 to 1995, Walmart crossing around 1990]

Sears' answer was to look for growth somewhere else. In 1981 it bought a stockbroker, Dean Witter, and a real estate company, Coldwell Banker. The plan was that you'd buy a drill, open a brokerage account and sell your house, all at Sears. People joked about it. Socks and stocks.

[REC 07 | 1980s | A 1980s department store with a brokerage desk set up between the appliance aisle and the paint counter, a man in a suit talking to a couple in windbreakers, flat fluorescent light | The broker slides a brochure across the desk; the couple glance at each other; camera static, medium-wide | 6]

It also launched a credit card in 1985 called Discover. That one, to be fair, worked.

But around 1990, the unthinkable happened. Walmart passed Sears as the biggest retailer in the country. Sears had been number one for most of the century.

[ARCHIVE: News coverage of Sears ending the big catalog, January 1993 | Sears catalog discontinued 1993]

In 1993 Sears cut tens of thousands of jobs, closed more than a hundred stores, and did something that felt like the end of an era. It killed the big general catalog. The wish book was gone.

## Chapter 4: The secret (10:00 to 12:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Did Sears have the internet before Amazon?"]

Now here's the part almost nobody knows.

[REC 08 | 1980s | A family in a wood-panelled 1980s den gathered around a beige home computer with a glowing monitor, the father typing, a dial-up modem on the desk | The father presses a key and the family leans toward the screen; camera handheld, slight drift | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Prodigy online service screens and ads, 1988 to 1993 | Prodigy online service 1990]

In the mid-1980s, Sears went into business with IBM on something new. An online service. You'd plug your home computer into the phone line and get news, weather, email, games, and shopping. You could browse products and order them from your living room. It was called Prodigy, and by the early nineties it had over a million members.

SAM (location) {walks between two sheet-covered mannequins and stops}: Now watch this. By the time Jeff Bezos started Amazon in 1994, Sears had a hundred years of catalogs, a national delivery network, and a stake in an online store.

SAM (location) {lifts the edge of a dust sheet and lets it drop}: It had everything Amazon needed. Except the belief that it mattered.

Prodigy was expensive, slow, and Sears saw it as a sideshow. In 1996 it sold its share. Two years earlier, a man in a garage in Seattle had started selling books online.

[REC 09 | 1990s | A 1990s strip-mall Sears-style catalog pickup desk, an older woman collecting a boxed order from a clerk, wood-grain counter, overhead fluorescent light, no logos | The clerk lifts the box onto the counter; she signs a slip; camera static | 6]

The machine that had beaten every general store in America, the one that brought the store to your house, had been built by Sears. And Sears walked away from it right before it mattered most.

## Chapter 5: The pieces (12:30 to 15:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did Sears fight itself?"]

[ARCHIVE: Eddie Lampert photos and news coverage, 2004 to 2013 | Eddie Lampert Sears]

In 2004 a hedge fund manager named Eddie Lampert, who already controlled Kmart, bought Sears for about eleven billion dollars. He put the two together and called it Sears Holdings. Together they had about thirty-five hundred stores.

Lampert was famous on Wall Street, a brilliant investor. And he had a theory. Big companies get lazy. So he'd break Sears into dozens of separate business units, tools, appliances, clothing, the website, each with its own boss and its own profits, and let them compete.

[REC 10 | 2000s | Executives in separate glass-walled offices along a corridor, each on the phone and gesturing, one slamming a folder down, cool fluorescent light, figures in silhouette | One man hangs up hard and stares at the next office; camera tracks slowly along the corridor | 6]

SAM (location) {gestures to the dark floor around him}: Imagine your kitchen and your bedroom fighting over the electric bill. That's roughly what happened.

The units fought over money, ad space and floor space. In some accounts, divisions refused to cooperate on promotions that would have helped the whole store, because it would help the other unit's numbers. Meanwhile, the stores got old. Leaky roofs. Broken escalators. Empty shelves.

[REC 11 | 2010s | A department store tool department in 2014 with half-empty red pegboards, a bucket under a ceiling leak, buzzing fluorescent tubes, one customer walking past | A drip lands in the bucket; the customer keeps walking; camera static | 6]

[GFX: Bars, Sears spending on store upkeep vs share buybacks, 2005 to 2014]

Sears spent billions buying back its own shares, and it spent far less fixing up its stores than rivals like Target and Walmart did. Then it started selling the pieces that worked. Lands' End. Sears Canada. The Craftsman brand, sold in 2017. And hundreds of its best store buildings went to a new real estate company that Lampert's own fund helped control, and Sears paid rent to be in them.

[GFX: Count-down, Sears Holdings stores 2005 to 2018]

The more it sold, the less there was to save.

## Chapter 6: The last day (15:30 to 17:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Is there still a Sears?"]

[ARCHIVE: Bankruptcy coverage, October 2018; closing-sale signs | Sears bankruptcy 2018]

On October 15th, 2018, Sears filed for bankruptcy. A few months later, Lampert's own fund bought what was left out of bankruptcy, a few hundred stores. Most of those closed too.

[REC 12 | 2010s | An empty mall department store entrance, the store dark behind a pulled-down steel security grille, mall corridor lights on outside, an empty bench | A mall security guard walks past the grille without looking; camera static, wide | 6]

Today there are only a handful of Sears stores left in America. The company that invented shopping from home lost to shopping from home.
