## Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)

[REC 01 | 1970s | A 1977 shopping mall electronics shop with a small crowd gathered around a beige personal computer with a small monochrome monitor on the counter, a clerk typing, warm tungsten and fluorescent light, no logos | The screen shows blocky white text; a teenager leans in over the clerk's shoulder; camera handheld behind the crowd | 6]

In 1977, if you wanted to buy a personal computer in America, one of the easiest places to do it wasn't a lab or a specialist shop. It was a little store in your local mall. RadioShack.

[ARCHIVE: TRS-80 Model I photos and 1977 RadioShack catalog pages | TRS-80 Model I]

It sold a computer called the TRS-80, and in its first years it was one of the best-selling computers in the country. RadioShack was, for a moment, one of the most important computer companies in America.

SAM (study) {holds a 9-volt battery between finger and thumb}: Thirty-eight years later, it went bankrupt, best known for asking you for your phone number when you bought one of these.

SAM (study) {sets the battery down}: I'm Sam. This is Last Receipt. Tonight, why RadioShack failed, and how it had the future and sold batteries instead.

## Chapter 1: The radio room (0:45 to 3:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Where did the name RadioShack come from?"]

[REC 02 | 1920s | A narrow 1920s radio parts shop in Boston, two men in shirtsleeves and waistcoats examining vacuum tubes at a wooden counter, shelves of boxed parts | One holds a tube up to the window light and squints at it; camera static, medium | 6]

It started in 1921 in Boston, as a store for radio hobbyists. The name came from the little room on a ship where the radio equipment was kept. The radio shack.

Two brothers opened it, at a time when radio was the most exciting technology in the world. People built their own sets at kitchen tables, from parts. You didn't buy a radio so much as assemble one, and the store that sold the parts was the center of that world. For decades RadioShack sold mostly by catalog, to amateurs and ship operators and anybody who liked to solder.

[ARCHIVE: Charles Tandy portraits; Tandy Corporation history, 1960s | Charles Tandy]

For forty years it was a small chain for tinkerers. Then, in 1963, a Texas businessman named Charles Tandy, who'd built a company selling leather craft kits, bought it when it was nearly broke. Tandy knew one thing very well: the hobby customer.

[REC 03 | 1980s | Close-up of a wall of small labelled drawers full of resistors and capacitors in a 1980s electronics shop, a hand pulling one drawer open, warm tungsten light | The drawer slides out with a rattle of tiny parts; camera static, macro | 5]

He made the stores small and put them everywhere. Malls, strip centers, small towns. He sold the store's own brands, cheaper than the big names. And he had a famous trick. A free battery card. Bring it in, get a free battery every month. Of course, while you were in for your free battery, you'd usually buy something else.

[ARCHIVE: Realistic, Archer and Optimus branded products, 1970s to 1980s | Realistic stereo RadioShack]

And those own brands were the quiet engine. Realistic stereos. Archer antennas. Optimus speakers. Most people never realized those were all RadioShack. Because Tandy controlled the brands, he controlled the profit. A big-name stereo might make a store a thin margin. A Realistic one made RadioShack a fat one.

[REC 04 | 1980s | A clerk handing a free battery across a store counter to a smiling 10-year-old boy holding a small card, 1980s, warm light | The boy takes the battery and holds it up; camera static, medium | 5]

SAM (study) {smiles}: Every kid who liked taking things apart grew up in that store. Resistors in little drawers. Kits that turned into radios. It was the place where you learned how things worked.

[ARCHIVE: RadioShack electronic project kits ("150-in-1"), 1970s to 1980s | RadioShack 150 in 1 electronic project kit]

Maybe you had one of those project kits. A board covered in springs, and a book of circuits. You'd push wires into the springs following a diagram, and suddenly you'd built a burglar alarm, or a radio, or a little siren that drove your parents mad. Thousands of engineers will tell you that's where it started for them. Not in a classroom. In a cardboard box from the mall.

## Chapter 2: The computer (3:30 to 6:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Did RadioShack start the home computer revolution?"]

[ARCHIVE: 1977 home computer trio coverage: Apple II, Commodore PET, TRS-80 | 1977 trinity Apple II PET TRS-80]

In 1977, three personal computers went on sale that changed everything. The Apple II. The Commodore PET. And RadioShack's TRS-80.

The TRS-80 was cheaper than the Apple. And RadioShack had something Apple didn't. Thousands of stores, already in every town, where you could walk in, try it, and take it home that day.

[REC 05 | 1980s | A teenager in 1983 typing code on a home computer in a wood-panelled bedroom, posters on the wall (no readable text), a cassette recorder plugged into the computer | He hits enter and waits, then grins at the screen; camera static over his shoulder | 6]

Tandy reportedly expected to sell a few thousand. They sold many times that. A whole generation of programmers wrote their first code on a TRS-80. People called it the Trash-80, with affection.

Charles Tandy didn't live to see most of it. He died in 1978, only a year after the TRS-80 came out. The man who'd understood the hobby customer better than anyone was gone right as that customer was about to change the world. Sit with that for a second, because it matters to everything that follows.

SAM (location) {walks behind the bare counter}: Now watch this. RadioShack had the stores, the brand and the customers who would build the computer age. The kids in those aisles became the engineers.

SAM (location) {taps the glass}: RadioShack just didn't build anything for them.

[ARCHIVE: First Apple Store opening, Tysons Corner, Virginia, 2001 | Apple Store opening 2001]

Here's an irony worth a moment. In 2001, Apple opened its first retail stores. Its big idea was a place in the mall where you could walk in, try the computer, and talk to someone who actually understood it. That's a good description of a RadioShack in 1977. Apple's stores became some of the most profitable shops on Earth, per square foot. RadioShack had been running that experiment twenty-four years earlier, in thousands of malls, and walked away from it.

[GFX: Market-share bars, home computers 1978 vs 1984: Tandy shrinking as Apple, Commodore and IBM-compatibles grow]

IBM launched its PC in 1981, and dozens of companies started making cheap copies. RadioShack kept making computers for a while, but it never led again. In 1993 it got out of making computers altogether.

To be fair, it had one more good run. In the mid-eighties, its Tandy 1000 was one of the most popular home computers that could run IBM software, and lots of families had one. But the company never treated computers as its future. They were one more department, like stereos and car antennas. When making PCs got hard and cheap, it simply sold the factories and moved on.

## Chapter 3: Your phone number (6:30 to 9:30)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did RadioShack want your phone number?"]

[REC 06 | 1990s | A cashier at a small electronics store counter asking a customer something, the customer in a 1990s windbreaker frowning, a single pack of batteries on the counter | The customer shakes his head and taps the batteries; the cashier shrugs; camera static over the cashier's shoulder | 6]

And then there was the phone number. For years, if you bought anything at RadioShack, even a single battery, the clerk asked for your name, address and phone number.

SAM (location) {holds up a single battery}: Why does a store need your phone number to sell you a nine-volt? Everyone asked. Nobody liked it.

The answer was the mailing list. RadioShack sent out catalogs and flyers, and the list was valuable. When the company went bankrupt, millions of those customer records were listed for sale with everything else, until state attorneys general stepped in.

Think about that from the customer's side. You stood at a counter, a little annoyed, giving a stranger your address so you could buy a fuse. Years later, that little act of politeness became an asset on a bankruptcy list. It's a small story. But it tells you how the company had started to see its customers. Not as builders. As records.

[ARCHIVE: Incredible Universe superstore photos, 1990s | Tandy Incredible Universe]

In the nineties, RadioShack tried giant superstores called Incredible Universe. They were huge, expensive, and closed within a few years. It tried selling computers made by other brands. Then it found something that worked. Cell phones.

[REC 07 | 2000s | A small electronics store in the 2000s filled with cell phone displays and remote-control cars, almost no customers, one clerk at the counter, bright fluorescent light | The clerk straightens a phone display and looks at the door; camera static, wide | 6]

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of RadioShack's email layoffs, 2006; CEO resignation, 2006 | RadioShack layoffs by email 2006]

And the mid-2000s were rough inside the company too. In 2006, RadioShack let about four hundred office workers go by email. The message said that their position was one that had been eliminated. That same year, its chief executive resigned after it came out that he'd claimed college degrees he didn't have. Neither story killed the company. Both told you something about it.

By the 2000s, phones and phone plans were a huge part of RadioShack's business. It was basically a phone store with a parts drawer in the back. And when you're a phone store, you're competing with the phone companies' own stores, and every big-box store on earth.

It's easy to see why phones were tempting. Every time a RadioShack clerk signed you up for a new plan, the phone company paid RadioShack a fee. Those fees were big, much bigger than the profit on a pack of resistors. So the floor space shifted. More phone displays at the front, fewer parts drawers at the back. The staff were trained to sell plans, not to explain how a transistor worked. Each change made sense that quarter. Together, they changed what the store was for.

## Chapter 4: The Shack (9:30 to 12:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Could a new name save RadioShack?"]

[REC 08 | 2000s | A bored employee leaning on an electronics shop counter, chin on hand, a wall clock behind him, empty store, 2009 | He sighs and checks the clock; camera static, medium | 6]

[ARCHIVE: "The Shack" rebrand ads, 2009 | RadioShack The Shack 2009]

In 2009, RadioShack tried to look young and cool. It started calling itself The Shack. Customers didn't buy it, and before long it quietly went back to the old name.

[REC 09 | 2010s | A shopper in an electronics store aisle comparing prices on a smartphone, holding a product box in the other hand, 2012 | He looks at the phone, puts the box back on the shelf and walks off; camera static, medium | 6]

Then, around 2010, something ironic happened. The tinkerers came back. A maker movement took off. Cheap little computer boards like Arduino and Raspberry Pi. 3D printers. Kids building robots. This was RadioShack's original customer, exactly.

[ARCHIVE: Maker Faire photos, 2010s; Arduino and Raspberry Pi boards | Maker Faire 2012]

These people wanted components, sensors, motors and wire. They wanted someone behind the counter who knew what a capacitor was. RadioShack did start stocking some of those boards and kits again. But the shelves were small, the staff had been hired to sell phones, and the makers had already found online shops that sold the same parts, cheaper, with instructions.

SAM (study) {shakes his head slowly}: And by then, RadioShack had pushed most of the parts drawers into a corner to make room for phone cases.

[ARCHIVE: RadioShack 2014 Super Bowl ad, "The '80s called" (stills) | RadioShack Super Bowl 2014 80s called]

[GFX: Side by side: "1977: one of the best-selling computers in America." "2012: phone accessories by the register."]

It even made fun of itself, in a Super Bowl ad in 2014. Characters from the eighties stormed a store and took all the old stuff away. The joke was: the eighties called, they want their store back. It was funny. It was also a confession.

## Chapter 5: Batteries not included (12:00 to 13:30)

[REC 10 | 2010s | Empty plastic parts drawers being tipped into a large bin by an employee in a closing store, rattling, overhead fluorescent light | The drawer empties into the bin; he reaches for the next; camera static, close | 5]

[ARCHIVE: RadioShack bankruptcy coverage, February 2015 | RadioShack bankruptcy 2015]

In February 2015, RadioShack filed for bankruptcy. Around two thousand stores were sold, many to be turned into phone stores. In 2017, what was left went bankrupt again. The name has since been passed between owners and pasted onto all sorts of things.

In a lot of small towns, the RadioShack had been the only place to buy a cable, a charger or a tiny screw for your glasses. Those didn't all disappear at once. Some were independent dealers who owned their own shops and carried on under the name, a little island of parts drawers in towns the big stores never reached.

Years later, the brand itself was bought by new owners who used its name for an online shop, and for a while, for a cryptocurrency project whose social media posts were so crude they made headlines. A name that once meant "this is where you learn how things work" ended up meaning almost nothing at all.

SAM (study) {rolls the 9-volt battery between his fingers}: Here's the thing about a name. It's only worth what it promises. When the promise is gone, you're just selling the letters.

## The verdict (13:30 to 15:00)

[GFX: Timeline recap: 1921 Boston radio store, 1963 Tandy buys it, 1977 TRS-80, 1993 out of computers, 2000s phone store, 2009 "The Shack", 2014 Super Bowl ad, 2015 bankruptcy, 2017 again]

Let's take it from the top. A store for radio hobbyists. A leather-kit businessman who put one in every town. One of the first home computers in America, sold to kids who'd become the engineers of the next forty years. And then decades of chasing whatever sold best that year, until the curious kids grew up and shopped somewhere else.

SAM (boardroom) {stands at the head of the table, a 9-volt battery in hand}: So what killed RadioShack? Amazon, partly. Big-box stores, partly. Phones, definitely.

SAM (boardroom) {sets the battery on the table}: But here's my verdict. RadioShack's real customers were the curious ones. The builders. It had them first, and it kept trading them for whatever was selling that year.

[GFX: Red CLOSED stamp lands on a TRS-80 photo. One heavy thud.]

SAM (boardroom) {looks into the lens}: The lesson: know who your weirdest, most loyal customers are. They're usually a few years ahead of everyone else.

SAM (boardroom) {small smile}: What did you build with something from RadioShack? Tell me in the comments. I want to hear about the kits.

SAM (boardroom) {nods toward screen right}: Next week, the bookstore that handed its future to Amazon. On purpose. Borders. It's right here.
