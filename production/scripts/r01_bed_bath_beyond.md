## Chapter 1: Bed 'n Bath (0:40 to 3:00)

[ARCHIVE: Warren Eisenberg and Leonard Feinstein photos; early Bed 'n Bath stores | Bed n Bath 1971 Eisenberg Feinstein]

It started in 1971 in New Jersey, as a little shop called Bed 'n Bath, opened by two men who'd worked together at a discount chain. They had a hunch that people were starting to care about sheets and towels as fashion, not just things you needed.

Warren Eisenberg and Leonard Feinstein had watched department stores treat linens as an afterthought, a back corner on an upper floor. They thought that was a mistake. In the seventies, colored towels and patterned sheets were becoming a way to make a home feel like yours. They opened in a strip mall, kept the prices sharp, and grew one store at a time.

[REC 02 | 1990s | A packed 1990s home-goods superstore with towering shelves of towels, pillows and kitchen gadgets stacked to the ceiling, shoppers pushing carts through, bright fluorescent light, no logos | A woman reaches up for a pillow on a high shelf on tiptoe; camera handheld, wide | 6]

In the late eighties they changed the name to Bed Bath and Beyond and built enormous stores. Stacked to the ceiling. If you needed a garlic press, a shower curtain and twelve towels, it was one trip.

[ARCHIVE: Bed Bath & Beyond superstore interiors, 1990s | Bed Bath Beyond store 1990s]

There was a reason everything went up to the ceiling. For years, the company barely used central warehouses. Suppliers shipped straight to each store, and the stock lived right there on the shelves, all the way up. It saved money. And the store managers had unusual freedom. They could choose much of what went on their own shelves, so a store in Florida and a store in Minnesota looked quite different. The founders trusted the people closest to the customers.

[GFX: Diagram: suppliers shipping straight to stores, no central warehouse]

[REC 03 | 2000s | A young couple in 2008 walking a home-goods store with a handheld registry scanner gun, scanning a stand mixer and laughing, bright light | He scans the box with a beep and she gives a thumbs up; camera handheld, medium | 6]

It became the wedding registry. The college dorm shopping trip. For about twenty years, it was one of the best-run retailers in America.

The company grew by buying other chains too. A chain of beauty and drugstores. A quirky New England discount store called Christmas Tree Shops. A world-goods importer, Cost Plus World Market. And a baby store, buybuy BABY, which became a big business of its own. Wall Street loved it. For years, Bed Bath and Beyond hit its numbers like clockwork.

SAM (study) {lines up the coupon square with the edge of the desk}: Remember the founders' instinct. Trust the store. Let the people in the aisle decide. Hold onto that, because the people who came later did the opposite.

## Chapter 2: The blue coupon (3:00 to 6:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "How can a coupon be dangerous?"]

[REC 04 | 2000s | A mail carrier walking a sunny suburban street in the 2000s, stuffing big blue postcards into a row of mailboxes, no readable text | She pushes a card into a mailbox and moves to the next; camera static, wide | 6]

And then there was the coupon. Big, blue, twenty percent off. It came in the mail, over and over. Bed Bath and Beyond took them even after they'd expired. It took competitors' coupons too.

At first it was brilliant marketing. The coupon was big enough that you couldn't lose it, and bright enough that you couldn't miss it. It got you through the door, and once you were in that huge store, you'd buy three other things at full price. For a while, that's exactly how it worked.

SAM (location) {walks along a bare shelf and stops}: Here's the thing about a coupon everyone has. It's not a discount anymore. It's just the price.

[REC 05 | 2010s | A long checkout line in a home-goods store where every customer is holding a blue coupon ready, a cashier taking one after another | The cashier takes a coupon, scans it, reaches for the next; camera static from the end of the line | 6]

[GFX: Physical number: "A large share of sales used a coupon. Every one of them was 20% straight off the margin."]

Customers learned to wait for the coupon before buying anything. So the company kept mailing them. It couldn't stop, because the moment it did, people would just wait.

Think about what that does to a business. Imagine a coffee shop that gives everyone twenty percent off, every day, forever. It isn't a promotion anymore. It's a price cut, paid for out of the profit on every single cup. And because it comes in the mail, the coffee shop is also paying for the printing and the postage, millions of times over.

And then the internet made it worse. Phone apps and websites made it easy for anybody to show a coupon at the register. The few people who'd been paying full price stopped.

## Chapter 3: Buying itself (6:00 to 8:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Where did the money go?"]

[REC 06 | 2010s | A boardroom with executives in silhouette applauding a rising chart on a screen (numbers blurred), city view behind | They clap; one stands; camera static from the back | 5]

Meanwhile, Amazon was selling towels, and Target was making its home section beautiful. Bed Bath and Beyond's stores looked the same as they had in 1995. Its website was clumsy.

The founders stepped back from running the company in the early two-thousands, and the man they'd chosen ran it for the next sixteen years. He was a steady hand, and for a long time the numbers stayed good enough. But the stores stayed the same. The inventory systems stayed old-fashioned. The online store lagged far behind. A retailer can coast for a long time on habit. Bed Bath and Beyond coasted for about a decade.

And instead of fixing all that, the company spent billions of dollars buying back its own stock. Over a decade or so, by many estimates, more than ten billion dollars.

[GFX: Bars: share buybacks vs spending on stores and technology, 2010 to 2019]

A buyback is when a company uses its own cash to buy its shares back from investors. With fewer shares around, each one is worth a slightly bigger slice, and the share price often goes up. It can make sense when a company truly has nothing better to spend on. Bed Bath and Beyond had plenty to spend on. It chose the share price.

SAM (location) {taps the bare shelf}: Now watch this. That's money that could have rebuilt every store and the website, twice. Instead it went to make the share price look better for a while.

## Chapter 4: The new brands (8:00 to 10:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did customers stop finding what they came for?"]

[ARCHIVE: Mark Tritton photos; coverage of the owned-brands strategy, 2020 to 2022 | Mark Tritton Bed Bath Beyond owned brands]

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of activist investors pushing for board changes, 2019 | Bed Bath Beyond activist investors 2019]

He arrived after a group of activist investors forced a shake-up of the board, frustrated by years of falling profits. They wanted change, and fast.

In 2019, a new chief executive came in from Target. His plan: replace many of the big national brands on the shelves with the store's own new brands, which make more profit per item.

[REC 07 | 2020s | Store shelves stocked with unfamiliar plain-label kitchen products, a customer frowning as she searches the shelf for a specific brand that isn't there | She takes out her phone, checks something, puts the phone away and walks off; camera static, medium | 6]

Customers walked in looking for the coffee maker they knew. It wasn't there. Something they'd never heard of was. They walked out and bought the one they knew somewhere else. At the same time, the shelves started looking empty, because the company was short on cash and suppliers got nervous.

The plan also took power away from the stores. Decisions about what to stock moved to headquarters, with new data systems and a cleaner, more modern look. On paper it was what every consultant would recommend. In practice it threw away the one thing the founders had got most right: a store manager who knew what their own customers wanted.

SAM (location) {lifts an empty box from the bottom shelf, finds it light}: You can tell a lot about a store by its bottom shelf. When it's empty, the money's already gone.

## Chapter 5: The meme and the end (10:00 to 12:00)

[REC 08 | 2020s | A young person in a dark bedroom at night watching rising stock charts on three monitors (numbers blurred), energy drink cans on the desk | They lean back and pump a fist at the screen; camera static from behind | 5]

In 2022, something strange happened. Bed Bath and Beyond became a meme stock. Online traders piled in. The share price shot up, crashed, shot up again. For a while the stock moved like a lottery ticket. The business underneath was still dying.

A well-known investor took a big stake, pushed for changes, and then sold all his shares that summer, at a profit, after the price had jumped. Many small traders who had followed him in were left holding shares that kept falling. The company even tried to raise money by selling new shares to all that excitement. It wasn't enough.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the Bed Bath & Beyond bankruptcy, April 2023 | Bed Bath Beyond bankruptcy April 2023]

In April 2023, Bed Bath and Beyond filed for bankruptcy. Every store closed. Its baby stores too.

The closing sales went on for months. Shoppers came in for one last deal and found shelves that were half bare, then a quarter full, then fixtures for sale. Couples who'd built their wedding registries there got emails telling them to use their gift cards quickly. The baby stores, which many parents had depended on, went with it, though the baby brand was later bought and brought back on a smaller scale.

Thousands of people lost their jobs. Many had worked there for years, through the good decades, when the company could seemingly do no wrong. They were the ones who'd watched the bottom shelves empty out before anyone on television noticed.

[REC 09 | 2020s | A cashier in a liquidating home-goods store shaking her head politely at a customer presenting a blue coupon, empty shelves behind them | The customer looks at the coupon, then laughs and puts it away; camera static over the shoulder | 5]

And yes. People were still trying to use the coupons right to the end.

[REC 10 | 2020s | A single blue paper coupon blowing across an empty store parking lot on a windy grey day | The coupon tumbles across the asphalt and out of frame; camera static, low | 5]

The name was bought by an online retailer and lives on as a website, and even a few new stores. But the company that built those stores is gone.

That online retailer liked the name so much it renamed itself Bed Bath and Beyond. Which tells you what was still valuable at the end. Not the stores, not the systems, not the shelves. The name, and the memory of a big blue coupon in millions of kitchen drawers.

## The verdict (12:00 to 13:30)

[GFX: Timeline recap: 1971 Bed 'n Bath, 1987 new name and superstores, 1990s to 2000s growth and the blue coupon, 2010s buybacks, 2019 new CEO, 2022 meme stock, 2023 bankruptcy]

Let's run it back. Two founders who saw that towels could be fashion. Huge stores run by managers who knew their neighbourhoods. A coupon so good it became the real price. A decade of spending on its own shares instead of its own stores. And then a rushed reinvention that took away the brands people came for.

SAM (boardroom) {stands at the head of the table, a blue coupon on the table}: So what killed Bed Bath and Beyond? Amazon helped. The meme stock circus didn't help. The new brands hurt.

SAM (boardroom) {taps the coupon}: But underneath all of it was this. It taught its customers that its own prices weren't real. Once you've done that, you can't take it back.
