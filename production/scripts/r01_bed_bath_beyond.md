## Chapter 1: Bed 'n Bath (0:40 to 3:00)

[ARCHIVE: Warren Eisenberg and Leonard Feinstein photos; early Bed 'n Bath stores | Bed n Bath 1971 Eisenberg Feinstein]

It started in 1971 in New Jersey, as a little shop called Bed 'n Bath, opened by two men who'd worked together at a discount chain. They had a hunch that people were starting to care about sheets and towels as fashion, not just things you needed.

[REC 02 | 1990s | A packed 1990s home-goods superstore with towering shelves of towels, pillows and kitchen gadgets stacked to the ceiling, shoppers pushing carts through, bright fluorescent light, no logos | A woman reaches up for a pillow on a high shelf on tiptoe; camera handheld, wide | 6]

In the late eighties they changed the name to Bed Bath and Beyond and built enormous stores. Stacked to the ceiling. If you needed a garlic press, a shower curtain and twelve towels, it was one trip.

[REC 03 | 2000s | A young couple in 2008 walking a home-goods store with a handheld registry scanner gun, scanning a stand mixer and laughing, bright light | He scans the box with a beep and she gives a thumbs up; camera handheld, medium | 6]

It became the wedding registry. The college dorm shopping trip. For about twenty years, it was one of the best-run retailers in America.

## Chapter 2: The blue coupon (3:00 to 6:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "How can a coupon be dangerous?"]

[REC 04 | 2000s | A mail carrier walking a sunny suburban street in the 2000s, stuffing big blue postcards into a row of mailboxes, no readable text | She pushes a card into a mailbox and moves to the next; camera static, wide | 6]

And then there was the coupon. Big, blue, twenty percent off. It came in the mail, over and over. Bed Bath and Beyond took them even after they'd expired. It took competitors' coupons too.

SAM (location) {walks along a bare shelf and stops}: Here's the thing about a coupon everyone has. It's not a discount anymore. It's just the price.

[REC 05 | 2010s | A long checkout line in a home-goods store where every customer is holding a blue coupon ready, a cashier taking one after another | The cashier takes a coupon, scans it, reaches for the next; camera static from the end of the line | 6]

[GFX: Physical number: "A large share of sales used a coupon. Every one of them was 20% straight off the margin."]

Customers learned to wait for the coupon before buying anything. So the company kept mailing them. It couldn't stop, because the moment it did, people would just wait.

## Chapter 3: Buying itself (6:00 to 8:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Where did the money go?"]

[REC 06 | 2010s | A boardroom with executives in silhouette applauding a rising chart on a screen (numbers blurred), city view behind | They clap; one stands; camera static from the back | 5]

Meanwhile, Amazon was selling towels, and Target was making its home section beautiful. Bed Bath and Beyond's stores looked the same as they had in 1995. Its website was clumsy.

And instead of fixing all that, the company spent billions of dollars buying back its own stock. Over a decade or so, by many estimates, more than ten billion dollars.

SAM (location) {taps the bare shelf}: Now watch this. That's money that could have rebuilt every store and the website, twice. Instead it went to make the share price look better for a while.

## Chapter 4: The new brands (8:00 to 10:00)

[GFX: Chapter card "Why did customers stop finding what they came for?"]

[ARCHIVE: Mark Tritton photos; coverage of the owned-brands strategy, 2020 to 2022 | Mark Tritton Bed Bath Beyond owned brands]

In 2019, a new chief executive came in from Target. His plan: replace many of the big national brands on the shelves with the store's own new brands, which make more profit per item.

[REC 07 | 2020s | Store shelves stocked with unfamiliar plain-label kitchen products, a customer frowning as she searches the shelf for a specific brand that isn't there | She takes out her phone, checks something, puts the phone away and walks off; camera static, medium | 6]

Customers walked in looking for the coffee maker they knew. It wasn't there. Something they'd never heard of was. They walked out and bought the one they knew somewhere else. At the same time, the shelves started looking empty, because the company was short on cash and suppliers got nervous.

## Chapter 5: The meme and the end (10:00 to 12:00)

[REC 08 | 2020s | A young person in a dark bedroom at night watching rising stock charts on three monitors (numbers blurred), energy drink cans on the desk | They lean back and pump a fist at the screen; camera static from behind | 5]

In 2022, something strange happened. Bed Bath and Beyond became a meme stock. Online traders piled in. The share price shot up, crashed, shot up again. For a while the stock moved like a lottery ticket. The business underneath was still dying.

[ARCHIVE: Coverage of the Bed Bath & Beyond bankruptcy, April 2023 | Bed Bath Beyond bankruptcy April 2023]

In April 2023, Bed Bath and Beyond filed for bankruptcy. Every store closed. Its baby stores too.

[REC 09 | 2020s | A cashier in a liquidating home-goods store shaking her head politely at a customer presenting a blue coupon, empty shelves behind them | The customer looks at the coupon, then laughs and puts it away; camera static over the shoulder | 5]

And yes. People were still trying to use the coupons right to the end.

[REC 10 | 2020s | A single blue paper coupon blowing across an empty store parking lot on a windy grey day | The coupon tumbles across the asphalt and out of frame; camera static, low | 5]

The name was bought by an online retailer and lives on as a website, and even a few new stores. But the company that built those stores is gone.
